Abstract
This article selects Spanish ribless vaults as the object of study and presents new
research findings derived from the analysis of formal aspects. Although these vaults
have been studied in depth, a notably important question remains that substantially
modifies the design proposed, as well as their stereotomy and construction. In
particular, the analysis of so-called transverse joints should be conducted in
parallel but not entirely so. This aspect implies that relevant trace and construction
hypotheses should be more nuanced and, in certain cases, simplified. In other
words, this will allow an even closer approach to these vaults and their architectural
processes.

Introduction

Spanish ribless vaults are an extremely distinct architectural type resulting from the
experiences of stonemasons in the late Gothic period. These architectural examples
of stonework, located mainly in the old kingdom of Aragon (specifically in Valencia,
except for specific examples in Assier, France), were built between 1440 and 1550
and presented a new architectural system, which replaced Gothic ribs with groins
(thus reformulating the very concept of Gothic, regarded as an architectural system).
Moreover, they are different from the classical groined vaults, geometrically
generated by the intersection of two cylinders.
These works have been the subject of many investigations in recent times. Studies
have examined the complex formal, geometric, stereotomic, and construction
questions posed by these vaults. The greatest problems that arise, then, are those
of the architectural concept and the construction process itself. In fact, all these
investigations have culminated in the formulation of graphic proposals which
reasonably provide a solution to the problem of conceiving, shaping and assembling
these compositions. The key to understanding these proposals, in general terms, is to

Evolution and Shape of the Ribless Vaults

Most of the former investigations have framed preserved ribless vaults
chronologically, but it is worth citing the works of Zaragozá Catalán (1988, 1997,
2000, 2010), Luisa Tolosa and Alba (1997a; b) and Chiva Maroto (2014). These
authors have practically provided all the preserved historic documentation enabling
us to establish a clear timeline; this has also been included in Navarro Camallonga
(2018,2020). Although this is not the goal of this article, it is necessary to conduct a
literature review.
This new architectural system was developed by the architect Francesc Baldomar
around 1440, following a royal commission to build the tomb of the King of Aragon,
Alfonso I, the Magnanimous. The architect was commissioned to design a large
chapel in the old convent of Santo Domingo in Valencia (Navarro Camallonga and
Díaz 2017, 2018). For that purpose, Baldomar displayed his best architectural skills,
thereby creating a new vault system (Fig. 1). However, this new construction type
required experimentation, and for this reason, Baldomar constructed, prior to the
chapel, a small test vault, above a portal in the wall of Valencia, the so-called Portal
de Quart (Natividad 2010) (Fig. 2a). After finishing the vault of the Royal Chapel
around 1455 (Fig. 2b), Baldomar did not employ this singular architectural type
again until the 1460s, when he built another small vault in the Cathedral of Valencia
(Fig. 2c). Most likely, other examples were constructed during that time, but these
are the only ones that have survived.
The continuity of this period was defined by the activity in Valencia of Pere
Compte, disciple of Baldomar, who built two examples of ribless vaults in an
emblematic building in the city, the so-called Lonja (Fig. 2d, e). This architect,
or stonemason master, did not limit himself to imitating his mentor but applied
the construction system to the creation of new forms more in accordance with
new tastes, closer to the Renaissance. These two cases date back to the end of the
fifteenth century and the beginning of the sixteenth century, and with them, the
ribless vault period in Valencia ended.
Finally, two more examples are found in the church of Saint-Pierre Assier, France
(Navarro Camallonga et al. 2018), dated to the first half of the sixteenth century,
prior to 1543, which could only and necessarily be explained through the Valencian

The Question of Construction

One of the main problems identified in the study of groin vaults is their construction.
Despite researchers’ efforts to reconstruct the building process, it is still very difficult
today to obtain a rough idea of the possible reality in the course of this work.
However, historical documentation can undoubtedly provide clues, especially
in the case of the Royal Chapel. In the preserved documents (all of an economic
nature), specific points are sequentially explained in detail, namely, the completion
of the foundations and walls, the preparation of the springings and the moment of
vault closure. The timber expenses for falsework and scaffolds are also assessed,
as well as certain other elements, such as a large crane to transport materials and
a large pole (a so-called ‘antenna’) (Zaragozá Catalán 1997: I, 35), probably to
perform large-scale tracing tasks.
This allows us to understand the development of the work, but it does not
specifically explain how the various construction processes were executed. In fact,
we can only formulate experimental hypotheses such as those shown in Navarro
Camallonga (2018: 170) and (Zaragozá Catalán 1997: I, 35) (Fig. 5), where the
construction of a scaffold (as small as possible) and a centring system (also as light
as possible) is proposed. Additionally, the use of a counterweight system allows the
structure to remain stable throughout the construction process. These three systems,
in fact, constitute the only means to avoid the construction of excessive scaffolds
and falseworks (unthinkable at the time). However, we should remember the fact
that Zaragozá’s proposal (1997) was derived from (Fitchen 1961), and he referenced
the work of previous authors (Lassaulx and Whewell 1830–1831). These data are
important since they refer to a way of formulating construction hypotheses typical of
the nineteenth century, which cannot always be extrapolated to the original context.
It is difficult to assess whether this system facilitated construction (the only
way to verify this would be to reconstruct a similarly sized ribless vault). It is also
challenging to determine whether counterweights, hooks, or unknown systems were
adopted at the time. In any case, it is clear that there was a centring/holding system,

Definition of Transverse Arches

Let us focus on the zones of divergence, where the observed joint disposition
appears to be structured (approximately) radially. This fact leads us to consider
the possibility that the stone blocks are also transversely structured as arches. In
addition, if we extrapolate this principle to the rest of the vault, the following is
observed: as most of the surfaces have little curvature, most of the joints would
continue to appear practically parallel (Fig. 9).
This finding leads us to highlight certain aspects. First, the planes
corresponding to the transverse joints would be neither entirely parallel nor
vertical. Second, the structure would function in a much more complex way.
Third, the vault stiffness would be high since the blocks could not shift (not even
during the construction process). Fourth, the counterweight system could be
simplified. Fifth, the proposed tracing and stonework processes need to be more
nuanced.
In summary, these new considerations of ribless vaults as being composed of
transverse arches provides a new field of research. In the following sections, the
main structural, construction and geometric aspects will be analysed, primarily
the latter two aspects.

Structural Behaviour

Until now, the concept that seems evident is the fact that each vault sector
functions formally and structurally as an arch. In other words, these vaults consist
of repeated segments, which structure their stereotomy in horizontal support
planes in the lower part (springins) and in inclined support planes in the upper
part (the vault itself).
Considering the arch structure of ribless vaults, it is reasonable to assume that
the structural forces move perpendicular to these planes, as shown in Fig. 10
(although, given the formal complexity, an analysis of the structural calculations
based on this principle would surely reveal more complex behaviours). However,
this is the starting point (structurally, geometrically and architecturally) to better
understand the nature of these complex vaults. The next step will be to integrate
the theory of transverse arches into this assumption.
Because the transverse support surfaces are not parallel in their entirety, the
approach is substantially modified. Although the consideration of the vault sector
as a semi-arch is not modified, it is necessary to incorporate the fact that the
ashlars aligned between the different groins could also function as a transverse
arch. In fact, if we visualize the problem (to more easily understand the issue), we

However, it is difficult to determine whether the presence of these transverse

In addition, the possibility should also be considered that the occurrence of nonparallel joints is due to assembly factors or the construction process. In other
words, it is possible that these transverse arches are not a formal feature previously
conceived but the result of decisions made during the assembly process (which will
be examined below).
The resulting proposal, in any case, is a mixed structural work of the whole
ensemble, similar to musculature, which again remarkably resembles the behaviour
of traditional Gothic vaults. This new vision of ribless vaults (structurally and
architecturally) does not nullify the innovation and transformation they represent
with respect to the previous vaults but rather reveals a similar structural behaviour.
These findings may lead to a new research field generated by adopting the
transverse arch principle as a starting point, in which structural calculations would
be essential.

Tracing Simplification and Geometric Control

An important research point that should be addressed, even partially, is the
application of the principle of transverse arches to the architectural conception
process.
As indicated in previous research (Rabasa Díaz 2000: 23–24), tracing processes
are closely linked to construction. The tracing is a geometric support to develop
cutting patterns and to control the geometry throughout the construction process
but is not the only system. In fact, it was common throughout the Middle Ages
to combine these geometric supports with building skills. Skilled workers were
required who were able to repeatedly build complex vaulted systems with barely any
help from graphic elements.
In the case of ribless vaults, the complexity and geometric control necessarily
require a drawn graphic system, which makes it possible to obtain, at least,
a sufficient part of the necessary cutting and construction patterns.
The strict integration of transverse arches into the tracing system of ribless vaults
implies a considerable increase in the geometric complexity (Fig. 13). Therefore,
it makes no sense to think that the graphic system alone could provide a complete
solution to the entire stereotomic and construction process. For this reason,
considering all aspects presented, it is necessary to establish the limits between the
use of a geometric graphic system and a formal control system in the construction
process itself.
Therefore, it is advisable to refer to the tracing systems proposed in Navarro
Camallonga (2018: 123) when certain strictly necessary elements cannot be obtained
directly from the construction itself. One of these elements is the set of circular
curves defining the groins (which replace the ribs) in their entirety. The second
group consists of the cutting patterns of each support plane between the blocks.
These elements require a major geometric apparatus, which are indispensable,
because of the rigorous dimensional control detected in the surveys.
In contrast, it is not feasible to determine the transverse cutting pattern of
each stone block on a drawn tracing. Similarly, it does not make sense (within

Architectural Concept

In this section, a reasonable overview is proposed of the design and architectural
conception processes to clarify and describe all the above aspects.
First, the master builder must necessarily design the groin geometry and partially,
or intuitively, the curvature of the surfaces. This process, i.e., the design, results in
a very specific graphic system, which consists of a detailed definition of the plan
that includes the arrangement of the linear structure defining all parts of the vault.
A graphic system only remains to be established through which the most complex
elements are obtained, i.e., the tracing, and the cutting patterns of the stone blocks
(this needs to be defined on paper in advance, but its definitive execution will occur
at the construction site).
All this allows, almost immediately, the construction process to proceed without
too much detail. All that is required is to reproduce the tracing systems at full scale
to be able to work on them in the development and assembly of the vault.
It is essential to understand that the actual shape of ribless vaults is not directly
designed but rather based on a linear structure (as in practically any example of
a Gothic vault) (Rabasa Díaz 2000: 194). Although the result is a complex set of
curved surfaces, in reality, it is still the result of the application of an ingenious
geometric linear system (Fig. 16). Ribless vaults are only governed by linear
structures (complex but linear), in contrast to what occurs during the Renaissance,
when architectural and vaulting surfaces were usually conceived in a specific
way (as cones, spheres, toroids, etc.). As such, the whole beauty of ribless vaults
should not be understood through the resulting formal complexity but through the
proportional relationships between the different linear elements that comprise the
conceptual structure.

Conclusions

A similar process to that proposed in this article must surely have occurred within
the context of the work of Baldomar and Compte, allowing the construction of these
particular and complex vaults. The experimental nature of certain examples, such
as the Portal de Quart vault, supports this supposition. The analysis of this work,
in fact, allows us to appreciate that architectural processes do not consist only of
geometric layouts. The graphic stage should be complemented by experimental
building work to finally achieve well-codified systematics, which makes it possible
to expand the scale and complexity in later works, such as the Royal Chapel of Santo
Domingo.
