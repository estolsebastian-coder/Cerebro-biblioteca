Abstract
During the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries, three-section cantilevered stone
stairways supported by vaults of complex warped shapes were built along Spain’s
Mediterranean coast. Presented here are five case studies. The geometric analyses
and the comparative study with the relevant treatise illustrate the existence of a
geometric construction method. The process involves handling elevation and plan
projections simultaneously to obtain the geometric center of the curves that delimit
these vaults. The simplicity of the geometric formula applied relies on the use of
dimensional references obtained from the stairwell measurements.

Keywords  Staircases · Warped vaults · Vault design · Spanish renaissance
architecture

Introduction

Staircases integrated with several straight sections over warped stone vaults
with matching intersections are a cultural fact preceding and inspiring their
seventeenth- to eighteenth-century masonry analogues. Some master builders
of the period, such as Alonso de Vandelvira (ca. 1575–1591) and Joseph
Gelabert (2011), pointed out the special difficulty of their design. The Valencia
Stonecutters’ Guild (1565) provisions stated that “only masters can trace them”
(Falomir 1996: 201). In fact, this problem is addressed by only a handful of

However, such difficulty also allowed the architect to stand out when masters

Euclidean geometry and a remarkable ability to achieve the harmony and visual
coherence of a project that must be conceived in three dimensions with the
rigorous fulfilment of certain geometric guidelines. The execution of the warped
surfaces of the ashlars, all different from each other, are suggestive of difficult
carving operations.
Although the stonecutters’ ability to avoid these problems with imperceptible
geometric methods is well known, this also implies remarkable technical
expertise. In this case, the masters solved complex problems of plane geometry
by fixing some difficult-to-determine variables as baseline data. They became
accustomed to the use of scalloped or basket-handle arches, that is, arches
integrated with segments of arcs with different centres harmonized with each
other (Huerta 2007). They even worked with two projections (plan and elevation)
at once, adjusting the criteria for drawing the arcs, bed joints, etc., to specific
graphic results.

Reference Models

One‑Flight Medieval Stairways in the Mediterranean Region

Mediterranean medieval noble houses were built around an inner courtyard with a
stone stairway providing access to the piano nobile. Their immediate precedent was
likely fortified architecture. The most ancient stairways of this construction type are
the one-flight stairways resting on a narrow pointed vault and leaning on a thick wall
(Zaragozá et al. 2019).
In the second half of the fourteenth century, vaults were lightened to cover wider
spans. They were lowered, and the run was occasionally fragmented into several
sections that, in the first stage, remained aligned. The most ancient stairways, dating
from the thirteenth century, are found in Apulia federiciana. That same pattern was
later adopted in Naples, Sicily, Mallorca, Catalonia and Valencia, the relevance
of each piece depending on its size and on the guardrails’ decorative richness
(Zaragozá et al. 2019).
The vaults of these early examples are of cylindrical shape and very simple
tracing (setting out procedure). To carve the voussoir, face templates or an arch
square were used. Additionally, employing segmental arcs also allowed for better
adjustment of the vault to the slope of the steps, saving stone and reducing the
weight (Fig. 3).
Later, the intermediate pillars were replaced by corbels, which disappeared as the
vaults’ spans increased and their shapes evolved. To adapt the stairwell for domestic
purposes or to lighten the load, either the opening or outer edge of the vault was
raised. This caused the cylindrical shape of the intrados of the ancient barrel vault
to adopt a shape that is closer to the cone, which subsequently evolved into warped
shapes.
The result of this first evolution was the capialzada stairway, one of the opening
curves of which is elevated, forming a tapering vault. Its intrados approaches a
cone when concentric curves are used on both ends, but this also gives rise to more
complex shapes. The first applications of the capialzada style to staircase vaults
took place in Valencia, as indicated by several contracts in which the adjective
capçalat or capalçat is used (Gómez-Ferrer and Corbalán de Celis 2004; GómezFerrer 2005) (Fig. 4).
In this variant, the tracing is further complicated by the fact that all the voussoirs
are differently shaped. The front arches, generally concentric, now have a different
chord length, thus requiring different face templates, producing a slight warping of
the transverse joints or bed joint planes. In practice, the key of the design lies in the
orientation criteria used to arrange those bed joints in the vertical projection.

which Gelabert called the capialzada de otra manera, or engauxida (skewed)

Late Medieval Staircases of Several Flights

At the end of the fifteenth century, one-flight courtyard staircases evolved into twosection types, to which a corridor with one or more lowered vaults on abutments or
corbels was added. This innovation forced the meeting points to match. It seems that
the solution reached full maturity with the staircases designed by Pere Compte for
the new courtyard of the Palacio de los Borja, in Valencia (1485). These structures
might be the first examples of three-flight C-shaped stairways with arrised vaults
and rounded intersections (Zaragozá et al. 2019: 15).
It seems that the arrised vault intersections were the first ones to emerge, inspired
by the Valencian groined vaults of the fifteenth century (Navarro and Rabasa 2018:
145). Cleverly, the edge’s directional curve was defined in true magnitude, with
an arbitrary tracing radius assigned to overcome the difficult encounter between
two warped shapes. In this way, the complex cutting of the corner pieces could be
undertaken by means of carving operations similar to those used for the rest of the
voussoirs.
On the other hand, rounded intersections admit several solutions. It is possible
to first conceive a flat vault and later give it curvature calculated on the basis of
the contour edges (Equizzi 2019: 94–97). Alternatively, as Portor pointed out (1708,

Multi‑Flight Staircases with Matching Intersections

In the sixteenth century, staircases gained importance as ceremonial and spatial
articulation elements. This development was conducive to the adaptation of the
three-flight late medieval format in two novel variants clearly indicative of a
compositional vocation and exhibiting notable technical flair. This process benefited
from previous contributions by the master masons Antoni Dalmau, Francesc
Baldomar and Pere Compte.
Around 1566, Joan Anglés tried for the first time to integrate all the innovations
of the courtyard stairs with matching intersections in the cloister staircase of the
Convento de Santo Domingo de Orihuela (Alicante) (Fig. 6a). This design was
then replicated by Joan de Ambuesa in the monastery of San Miguel de los Reyes
in Valencia (1580–1583) (Fig. 6c) and later refined by Francisco Figuerola at the
Corpus Christi seminary in the same city (1598–1601) (Fig. 6b). The latter is the
best of the series (Marín and López 2018: 11).
In 1600, Joan Cambra composed two symmetrical three-flight, C-shaped
courtyard staircases, creating a pre-imperial layout for the new staircase designed for
the southern courtyard of San Miguel de los Reyes. According to the work contracts,
the first exploratory stage in the search for new types of staircases dates to the time
when Alonso de Covarrubias was in charge of the works (about 1548). Years before,
an even more effective solution had been proposed by Joan de Ambuesa for the
claustral staircase in the cloister’s northeast flank that consisted of symmetrically
doubling the staircase that is preserved today in the north tower.
This unexecuted project started an in-depth debate that remained latent until the
eighteenth century. This is the context for the staircase of the Casa Aduana Real
of Valencia (1762), currently the headquarters of the Superior Court of Justice
(Fig. 6d). The Casa Aduana Real was a building designed by Felipe Rubio y
Mulet (about 1767) in fulfilment of the requirement for being named an honorary
academician of the Academy of San Fernando in Madrid (Bérchez 1993: 134). The
building is considered to be the most important work of the Valencian eighteenth
century, housing significant stonework by Antonio Gilabert, to whom is attributed
the project of the two inner staircases surrounded by a wall and symmetrically
arranged in the central hall (Bérchez 1987: 10–12).
Tomás Vicente Tosca (1727: 250–252, stamp 18), presbyter, mathematician and
treatise writer, also reflected on this technical problem, which remained unsolved
in the architecture of the times and was directly linked to his concern for the
application of mathematics to the science of stereotomy.
The outstanding examples cited encouraged the dissemination of this typology
throughout Spain and as far as France (Zaragozá 2013). The connection of Valencia
with some Castilian examples, such as the staircase of the Real Chancillería de
Granada (Salcedo 2017) or that of the Monasterio de Uclés (Cuenca), remains
unsolved. The French case is limited for now to an example with a clear Valencian

Working Methodology

The present research entailed several working phases. In the first, an inventory
of this variant of stairs was compiled. In the old kingdom of Valencia, there are
five exceptional examples. Of these, the most ancient is found in the Monasterio
de Santo Domingo de Orihuela, and the others are preserved in Valencia: the two
exemplars (one claustral and one imperial) at the Real Monasterio de San Miguel
de los Reyes of Valencia; the claustral stairway of the Real Colegio Seminario de
Corpus Christi and the double claustral stairway of the ancient Casa Aduana Real,
which is the most recent.
Next, we carried out a graphic survey of the stairways as a necessary step for
subsequent geometric analysis (Almagro 2019: 5). A Faro 3D terrestrial laser
scanner (TLS), Focus 3D-130 model, was selected to overcome the constraints
posed by the complex shapes of the vaults. The low weight (5.2 kg) and small size
(24 × 20 × 10 cm) of this scanner simplified its use and transport. The device also
includes an integrated 70 megapixel camera, allowing photo-realistic point clouds
to be obtained, and its FLS files are compatible with all the processing software.
Its systematic distance error (2 mm in 25 m) is acceptable for the purposes of this
study. The scanner provides a high number of easy-to-use points for the subsequent
analysis of the tracings (setting out procedure) and, in addition, allows the scaling of
orthophotos, enabling direct geometric analysis.
A scanning station was established on each staircase landing to ensure good
overlap of the shots and correct registration of the cloud points, without blind spots.
The processing of the scans was performed using Scene version 19 (Fig. 7).
Graphic analysis was performed, since it allowed for recognition of the original
geometry (Spallone 2019: 80). The possible tracing was empirically determined
directly from the point clouds following the premises provided in the stonecutting
manuscripts by Vandelvira (1575–1591), Portor and Castro (1708) and Tosca
(1727), the only Spanish authors dealing with this type of stairway. Furthermore,
this method allows for better weighting of design errors, deformation and collapse,
and an exact exploded view of the ashlars.
Based on the previous work, a hypothesis was drawn up for a graphic tracing
for the two outer longitudinal sections of each run, assuming the existence of a
common pattern applying to all of them. Next, the curvature of the transverse
sections of the intrados or capialzo was determined. The definition of the curves

To discern the setting out method followed by Anglés, we proceeded to simulate
all the operations that the designer must have followed when projecting the
staircase. Starting by distributing the steps and creating the existing landing planes,
which Anglés most likely considered previously, and following the treatise sources,
we traced the auxiliary line joining the “teeth” of said steps and its parallel a foot
away. Due to Joan Anglés’s origins, we used the Tarragona palms (1 Tarragona
palms = 0.195 m) as the measuring unit. Additionally, we were able to ascertain
that the arches were effectively projected in ascending order, starting with the first
vaulted flight. For the starting flight, the tracing was made on the basis of three
pieces of information fixed by the master: the width of the vault on its outer edge,
which is one foot thick; the radii of curvature of the outer edge and the wall’s arches,
which are always the same and equal to half the width of the enclosing wall; and the
tracing centre, which is located at the midpoint of the next flight. With these data,
the two arches delimiting the vault are drawn to obtain the corresponding templates
and formwork. The vault’s thickness is fixed by drawing a parallel line one foot from
the auxiliary line joining the “teeth” of the steps.

modified. In this case, the tracing centre of the circumference is not a given piece
of information but the solution to the graphic problem. It should be kept in mind
that each vault must encounter the vault from the previous section at a point located
at the turning of two consecutive runs. Therefore, the vault’s starting point is one
of the set initial conditions. The remaining two, as in the first case, are the depth
of the vault (one foot thick) and the radii of curvature of the outer edge and the
wall’s arcs (inner and outer string), which in this instance are equal to the width

Staircase of the Colegio Corpus Christi

The staircase of the Colegio Corpus Christi (Fig. 6b) is the most relevant example
of the series due to its exquisite execution technique and larger scale. It is
enclosed in a tower with an almost square plan of 7.82 × 7.42 m (approximately
26 × 24.5 Valencian feet; 1 Valencian foot = 30.2 cm) (Table 2), delimited by
rammed earth Valencian walls 21.2 m high and 0.90 m thick. It is composed of
six flights (the usual number was three), five of which are cantilevered, and it was
built in two phases. The first five flights were designed by Francisco Figuerola
between 1598 and 1601, while the last flight was added in 1602 by Joan Baixet,
Joan María and Bartolomé Abril to give access to the library (Benito 1981: 76).
Most of the vaults give rise to forceful arrised intersections evoking Baldomar’s
groin intersections. The only exception is the connection with the corridor, as it is
constrained by the encounter with the capialzado vault.
The methodology used here was the same as that used for the Santo Domingo
stairway, verifying that both of them maintain the tangency of the well string of
the vault to the straight line located one foot from the auxiliary line joining the
“teeth” of the steps. This ensures the minimum thickness of the vault, regardless
of the metrological system used. In both cases, the width of the area (of the
staircase) is used as the curvature radius of the well and wall strings, except for
the first vaulted flight, whose radius is half of that.
As a unique feature, the wall curves at the third and fourth sections are
integrated by two tangent arcs with different radii since the design of the
transverse or bed joints of these staircases has utilized a directional curve. It
seems that the centres of these arcs were arbitrarily obtained on the elevation
itself, given that they coincide with singular points of it; this even suggests a
compositional intention, perhaps prompted by the presence of two doorways.
Figuerola, however, had the ability to place these centres on the segment joining
the point of tangency of both curves with the centre of the greater arc, ensuring a
perfect encounter.
Likewise, the radii of the bed joints also seem to have been defined directly,
without mediation of a previous tracing, adopting the same values as the
directional curves of each of the affected sections’ voids (Fig. 11).

Imperial Staircase of San Miguel de los Reyes

The Imperial staircase of San Miguel de los Reyes (Fig. 6c) is highly valued for
its innovative composition, although at a technical level, it does not offer major
developments. Despite being built shortly after those at the Orihuela and the
Colegio del Corpus Christi, its repertoire of possibilities is less refined, and it
seems to have taken its courtyard predecessors as direct technical referents.
The staircase is enclosed in a rectangular box measuring 7.37 × 17.15 m
(35 × 82 Castilian palms; 1 Castilian palm = 20.9 cm.). It starts with a blind
central flight, from which the two return runs arise.
The graphical analysis indicates that the two runs show some differences.
While the section on the left (Table 3) follows the criteria and methodology used
in the two previous stairways, in the section on the right (Table 4) the dimensions
are altered, which suggests that it was designed by a different architect (López
González et al. 2019).
Overall, it suggests an inadequate command of the technique, affecting both the
projection and the carving process. Its master did not manage to skilfully sort out
the constraints involved in adapting this staircase to a space with several accesses
and circulation paths. It seems that he used only the projection of the elevation to
trace the voussoirs. This resulted in very steep and irregular transverse courses,
to which many other imperfections were added, such as the disorganized rake
of the voussoirs’ radial joints. This, in addition to other facts, leads us to think
that its ashlars might have been laid out on site through a process similar to that
used for the severies at La Lonja de Valencia, as later detailed by Gelabert (2011:
151v–154v) (Fig. 12).

Staircase of the Aduana Real de Valencia

The tracing of the Staircase of the Aduana Real de Valencia (Fig. 6d) is attributed
to Antonio Gilabert (Bérchez 1993: 134), who might have been inspired by an old
abandoned project for the monastery of San Miguel de los Reyes. It is a three-flight
double stairway enclosed in a square plan of 6.80 m on each side (30 Valencian
palms; 1 Valencian palm = 22.65 cm) (Table 5).
The technical analysis reveals Gilabert’s acquaintance with the project criteria
followed by its predecessors, built 160 years earlier. He undertook the design as an
academic exercise of the highest mathematical rigor. As Tosca previously suggested,
it seems that Gilabert took into consideration both the floor plan and the elevation
projections simultaneously to achieve a coherent geometric construction. This could
perhaps serve as a general project rule for this typology (Fig. 13). The bed joints are
perpendicular to the wall, and the ashlars are regular in size. Only three different
voussoir layouts were used on the straight runs, a distribution that unavoidably had
to rest on the plan.

Conclusions

The results obtained attest to the existence of a formal control method for these
stairways’ vaults not collected in the known treatise sources. The procedure is based
on the use of references obtained from the enclosing wall dimensions because these
are easily remembered measurements and doing so allows the tracing to be done on
the staircase floor.
It should be noted that the design of highly complex combined surfaces can be
solved through a simple geometric procedure. By fixing some variables as baseline
data, all the curves of the voussoirs’ edges are defined. These curves are the only
reference that the stonemason has when warping the intrados surface. This last
operation, therefore, is remarkably difficult (Marín and López 2018: 7).
The forced visual continuity that should be given to these curves, extending them
through the lower part of the following section vaults to reach the wall, justifies the
definition of the pronounced curved sides so characteristic of these staircases, with
the exception of the first flight, where they are straight because this constraint is not
present.
