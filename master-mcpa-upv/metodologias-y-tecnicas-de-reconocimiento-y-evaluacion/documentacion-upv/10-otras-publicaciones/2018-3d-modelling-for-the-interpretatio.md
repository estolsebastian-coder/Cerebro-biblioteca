1.1 Introduction
The phenomenon of pirate attacks was present
on the Mediterranean coasts almost from the very
origin of maritime navigation. In the area of the
Ancient Kingdom of Valencia (Spain), and especially in the rocky coasts of La Marina (coastal
region north of the province of Alicante), the
attacks were considerably intensified in the first
half of the 16th century, being especially ferocious
in 1550, as a result of the alliance between Turkish
and Berber pirates.
The network of defense towers erected on the
Valencian coasts during the second half of the
16th century is a result of the geo-socio-political
context of the time, of the humanistic spirit of the
period and of factors related to progress in defense
systems and technical military matters. They are
part of a project intended to establish a coastal
defense and watchtower system, and whose main
objective was to warn, discourage and repel the
frequent pirate attacks on villages near the coast.
Emperor Charles V (1500-1558) impelled it and
his successor to the crown, Philip II (1527-1598),
ordered and executed it.
Coastal fortifications have long constituted a
fundamental control and security system for the
Mediterranean population. On the one hand, their
location and defensive structure required functional and architectural competence regarding the
Possibility of sighting, resisting, attacking and
Protecting the territory, and communicating with
the different fortified points of their surroundings.
On the Other hand, they transmitted the image of
a safeguarded land ready to fight pirate vessels, at
the same time symbolizing the power of the State
whose frontier they defended, In addition, over
time, the tower system has assumed a strong cultural identity component on the Mediterranean
coastline, characterizing the coastal landscape and
presenting features full of meaning, which today
stand as fascinating historical testimonies.
At present, the towers ensemble constitutes an
important architectural heritage that has Iost its
main characteristic: unity. Its current reality is
diverse. Many are Still preserved, in better or worse
state, but some have been Iost with no possibility
of being recuperated; a good number of them are
on privately owned land and are inaccessible.

1,1 Description
The area of study is a pre-Saharan valley to the
south of the High Atlas Mountains, between the
towns of Goulmima and Tinerhir. In the past,
these territories were pastures for cattle, mostly
ovine (goats and sheep) and bovine cattle of
nomadic populations. With sedentarization, the
oases were created: arable lands by artificial irrigation, surrounded by arid or desert territory. Irrigation combines the system of dams and ditches
(seasonal waters) with wells that supply irrigation
channels with water when the river becomes dry
on the surface. In some areas, a system of collection and transport of groundwater from points far
from the rivers, through khettaras, is added to this
mixed system. Traditional crops combine palm and
olive trees with cereals (Wheat and barley) and legumes (alfalfa) for pastures.
Nowadays, two ways of life continue to coexist
in Southern Morocco: the nomadic and the sedentary. Historically, the nomadic population has
been considered more powerful. Their control over
the handling of weapons and the ease in which
they adapt to the environment has allowcd them
to maintain control over sedentary populations,
obtaining everything they needed through looting, trading or simply in exchange for protection
against rival tribes (Rodríguez & Gil, 2015).
The characteristic habitat of the area is the ksar
(plural = ksour): a closed and fortified settlement
Where the population seeks refuge from external
attacks, usually coming from the nomadic population. It is built using traditional techniques and
employing local materials: raw soil in walls or

mud bricks, palm tree and tamarisk Wood, reeds
or oleander stems. The Khorbat is one of the main
ksour in the Ferkla Oasis, formed by two differentiated nuclei: the Old town (igrem akedim), already
