Traditional Structures Made with Gypsum Pillars:
A Reasoned Hypothesis
Fernando Vegas, Camilla Mileto, Maria Diodato,
José García Soriano, Carles Grau Giménez
Rincón de Ademuz is a parish in the region of
Valencia, Spain, historically isolated by the surrounding mountains. The economy has been traditionally characterised by the scarcity of means.
The only important activity that kept this parish
in touch with the exterior was transport of timber down the river in order to supply the City of
Valencia with wood for building needs (Diodato
2009). According to the existing documents,
this trade already existed 1,000 years ago during
Islamic domination and it only stopped during
the first half of the 20th century. T his circumstance has historically created a paradox: despite
the abundance of pine woods, timber was scarce
in the parish as it was mainly sent to the City to
be sold.
Villages and hamlets in the parish were built on
the southern slopes of the mountains for defensive reasons as well as to avoid occupying the fertile areas of the River Turia. For this reason, most
of the houses have two accesses at different levels,
one in the front façade giving on to the ground
floor and the Other in the rear façade at first floor
level or even higher.
The lower storey of the building has at least
one wall built directly touching the ground.
Thus, the structural system of this vernacular
architecture is based on a basement built with
stone masonry bonded with Clay mortar on the
ground floor, often serving as a containment wall
as well. A structural system based on gypsum pillars interconnected by beams and braces springs
from this stone basement (Fig. 1). The surrounding stone basement holds the first floor while
the gypsum pillars support the rest of floors and

the roof. Floors are built with gypsum-poured
jack vaults with a single vaulted formwork that
changes position during the building process as
the gypsum dries (Vegas et al. 201 Oa). The roof
is built with beams, rafters, crossed reeds [more
rarely wooden planks] and roof tiles bonded with
Clay mortar.

In the upper floors the façades are built with
thin walls between pillars, erected with several
techniques. The most common are five to ten
cm thick walls built with stone slabs vertically set
with gypsum mortar against a one-sided formwork, either plastered or unplastered (Sanz Arauz
2009; Mileto et al. 2011). T his system is also used
to build the partition walls. In Other, more recent
cases, a double formwork is filled with gypsum
and pieces of stone to build a ten to 12cm thick
wall. In some places, 20cm thick rammed earth
walls are erected either with or without gypsum
reinforcement (Vegas et al. 2009). The interior
fixed furniture such as chimneys, grain deposits,
kitchen shelves, etc. is built entirely with stone
slabs and gypsum. As is usual in Other places, interior plastering is also done with gypsum.
It may be noticed that all the vernacular architecture in Rincón de Ademuz is based upon the
general and almost exclusive use of gypsum as a
building material. Lime has never been employed
in the parish, except for building some churches
or priest's houses. But, even in some of these cases,
the use of lime cannot help being subsidiary to
the use of gypsum, due to the power of tradition.
For example, the priest's house at Castielfabib,
one of the more important villages in the area, is
built with walls made of rammed earth and lime
reinforced by gypsum, the latter always used to
provide structural strength. But why is gypsum
so widely employed in the area? And what is the
reason for this absolute confidence in the performance of the material?
Gypsum
The main reason for the employment of gypsum
in vernacular architecture in the area is its availability and abundance. Secondly, it may be considered that firing gypsum required 24 hours at
most, while firing limestone took four days. T his
circumstance meant that gypsum kilns required a
quarter of the firewood of lime kilns. And despite
the parallel presence of local limestone, this factor
was decisive in an area where the priority was to
float all the available wood down the river to be
sold, to such an extent that only small branches,
twigs and bushes where used as fuel in the gypsum kilns. Finally, proper use of gypsum during
building could guarantee exceptional performance
in strength and durability. Water was mixed in
varied but always small proportions according to
the element to be built, since less water meant
greater strength of the gypsum.l Another fundamental rule was to protect the gypsum from
ground damp with stone basements and to Shield
it from rainwater with generous roof eaves.
Gypsum kilns were simple cylinders erected
with dry stone masonry one-two m. in diameter and approximately two m. high, they were
built on the ground and sometimes dug into a
slope. In this second case, loading and unloading processes, as well as control of the firing process were facilitated by access to the upper part
of the kiln. The process begins with quarrying
gypsum stones by perforating the rock and hammering iron or wooden wedges inside in order to
break off pieces of gypsum stone. If the gypsum
kiln already existed, it was directly reused. If it
was to be built, it was marked out on the ground,
preferably on a slope as described above, and the
cylinder was built out of stone masonry, leaving
a gap in the front. If possible, the gypsum kiln
was placed directly on the rock ground. There
were two types of gypsum kilns: those placed near
the quarry and those placed near the building to
be erected. In the first case, there was no need
to transport the gypsum stones, but the gypsum
powder had to be transported anyway. In the second case, the gypsum stones were transported to
the building site and the gypsum was fired in situ.
In both cases, it was necessary to collect timber for
the firing process and take it to the gypsum kiln.
Gypsum firing was usually paid for by the family
building the house.
Before loading the kiln, the interior was rendered with Clay in order to prevent the heat from
escaping through the joints of the masonry wall.
Afterwards, the loading of the kiln was begun with
the construction of a false dome at the lower part
of the cylinder made with gypsum stones in order
to create the space for the fire. A mouth was built
in this dome to feed the fire. Once the dome was
closed, the cylinder was filled with gypsum stones
and the gap was walled. Last, the rest of the gypsum pieces and dust were thrown on it in order to
seal the interstices between the stones and control

On a suitable day to light the fire in the kiln,
i.e., when the firewood was dry, the wind was
calm and no rain was expected, the walled cylinder gap was rendered with Clay to prevent the
wind from entering and the heat from escaping
and the fuel was introduced and lighted. The firing process could last about 24 hours and it was
initiated in the afternoon because it was easier to
control the fire in the dark of night.
At the beginning, the kiln needed to be fed
continually in order to raise the temperature
quickly. During the process, the stones became
dark. The temperature of the kiln was maintained
during the whole process with the help of a long
Stick used as a poker on the firewood inside. The
nights spent feeding the fire were often an excuse
for parties with neighbours and friends, and the
fire was used to roast potatoes or meat.
The firing process was controlled by throwing
straw on the upper part. If it burned immediately, it meant that the stone was already fired in
that area and earth was thrown on it to divert the
flames to Other areas Still to be fired. The intensity and situation of the flames showed whether
the gypsum stones were properly fired or not.
Whenever this moment arrived, abundant earth
was thrown on the upper part and, afterwards,
the mouth of the kiln was walled with stones and
rendered with clay.
The kiln was left to cool down slowly and after
approximately ten days, the gap in the cylinder
was broken to extract the fired gypsum. If there
was a Clear area in front of the kiln, the ground was
cleaned, prepared and hardened before spreading
out the fired stones, which were crushed with a
hammer and were ground with the help of a millstone and a mule. In Other cases, the fired stones
were transported to a clearing or to the building
site itself, where it was ground in a similar way to
prepare it for use.
Once the grinding was finished, the gypsum
powder was collected, sieved and put in sacks,
ready to be used. If the gypsum stones had been
fired too much, then they were ground less, and if
they had not been fired much, they were ground
more. The man in charge knew how to fire the
gypsum stones according to the intended use of
the gypsum in construction. But, beyond that, the
most important factor to determine the structural
performance was to mix gypsum with only 50%
water or even less.

Study of the gypsum pillars
One of the most fascinating aspects of the use
of gypsum in the architecture of the area is its
employment to build structural pillars in buildings up to four floors high on some occasions.
They are square-shaped pillars built with stone
and gypsum in variable proportions: the proportion of gypsum in these pillars varies from ten to
50% of the total volume of the pillar. In the first
case, they are more masonry pillars bonded with
gypsum. The greater the proportion of gypsum
used, the more we may speak of true gypsumpoured pillars with a stone filling. It may also be
established that the greater the proportion of gypsum used, the less attention paid to bonding the
stones may be found when observing inside the
pillars, and vice versa. Except for the cases with a
very low proportion of gypsum, all pillars have a
monolithic appearance caused by the gypsum rendering applied to the surfaces.
This structure made of gypsum pillars acquires
great stability thanks not only to the wooden beams
supported by the pillars but also to the perpendicular wooden braces that connect them spatially. It
may be asserted that the structural performance and
state of conservation of the great majority of the pillars in the area, even the ones more than 200 years
old, are optimal and present no problem at all. Some
preliminary tests made on them showed a surprising
structural performance of 15 MPa and more (Bianco
and Guerrisi 2005, 109). Besides, the area has always
suffered from slight seismic movements, showing the
emciency of this vernacular structural system against
these small earthquakes.
Two of the authors of this text restored an Old
four-stosrey high road inn and converted it into a
modern four-star hotel, with all the structural and
functional requirements needed. Even in this case,
a detailed structural analysis of the existing gypsum pillars led to the conclusion that they should
be maintained as they were without any structural
reinforcement (Vegas et al. 2010b).

Springing of the pillars
Given the high hygroscopicity of gypsum, two
preventive measures are taken in order to avoid
humidity. First, pillars do not generally spring
directly from the earth and secondly, they are protected from the rain by ample eaves. In fact, the
majority of pillars spring from a stone masonry
wall bonded with Clay mortar (Fig. 2). The most
common [46% of the pillars studied] are the ones
that spring from a common masonry wall, in pillars placed both at the corners and in the middle
of the façade. In this case, the pillars continue the
structure vertically with the same building technique and bonding of the masonry wall, using
gypsum instead of Clay as bedding mortar and,
possibly plastering the pillar's external and internal surfaces (Fig. 3).4
A second similar but slightly different case is
the gypsum pillars that spring from stone masonry
containment walls in the case of semi-excavated
buildings on a slope [38% of the pillars studied]
(Fig. 4). Evidently, we may find gypsum pillars
that belong to the first type and gypsum pillars in
the rear façade that belong to the second group in
the same building. Last, there is a third case where
pillars spring directly from the earth [16% of the
pillars studied]. Though this solution is not apparently recommendable due to the hygroscopicity
of gypsum, it is true that in some cases this solution is almost inevitable on the ground floor or

Building the pillars
The perfect and even finishing of the surfaces of
the gypsum pillars in the area would apparently
suggest that formwork was used to erect them.
But, the frequent presence of beams or even stones
that appear out on the surface would initially contradict this hypothesis in most cases. Besides, the
employment of formwork to build the pillars
would be visible on the surface by means of the
traces of the wooden planks, as is the case of the
stone-slab façade walls built and plastered against
a one-sided formwork described above. Rain eroFig. 4: A gypsum pillar springing from a masonry containment

Rendering the gypsum pillars
After erecting the pillars, local builders used to
render them by pressing gypsum on their faces.
Gypsum permeated all the holes and interstices
perfectly. Nowadays, the traces that the wooden
listels left on the rendering may Still be noticed
in the corners of most of the pillars in the area, as
chey also served as a guide for applying it (Fig. 7).
In fact, these marks are Still visible in 84% of
the pillars studied. Very few pillars without traces
have been found and this may mean that the listels in these cases were only used as a guide while
bonding the stone masonry and were removed
soon afterwards.
The rendering was applied by using one of
three techniques. Although cheap, hand rendering was not the most common due to its rough
finish. It represents only 13% of the pillars studied. T his rendering was mainly used to render the
pillars in granaries and outhouses, although it may
also be found in dwellings. In this case, gypsum
was thrown on, manipulated and spread simply
by hand.
The most common rendering of the pillars
while building vernacular architecture in the area
was applied with a shovel. T his rendering represents 66% of the cases studied. More elaborate than hand rendering, its finish was flat and
smooth, although curved traces of the movement
of the shovel by the bricklayer may be found.
Lastly, smooth, even renderings without any
trace oftools were found. These represent 21% of
the pillars studied. They are very fine renderings
made by applying and pressing a large amount
of gypsum on the face of the pillar to be rendered and sliding a wooden board along the listels
in order to create a perfect smooth, continuous
surface.
The current idea of gypsum as an auxiliary
and secondary building material in architecture due to its hygroscopic condition and the
poor consideration of its structural performance
do not address the historical examples of vernacular architecture in certain areas of Spain,
where gypsum played an important role in

Besides the proper firing of the stone, the only
