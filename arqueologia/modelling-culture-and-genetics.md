Abstract

The rapidly expanding availability of palaeogenomic data provides new opportunities for
archaeological chronology, yet genetic evidence is rarely incorporated independently within
Bayesian chronological models. Here, we develop an approach for modelling cultural and genetic
transitions separately, allowing their temporal relationship to be evaluated explicitly. We apply this
framework to the Mesolithic-Neolithic transition in Denmark using 494 radiocarbon dates from 130
archaeological sites integrated with archaeological, isotopic and palaeogenomic evidence. Consistent
with previous research, the preferred model places the rapid establishment of Neolithic cultural
practices across Denmark around 4000 BC. The model further indicates that Mesolithic lifeways
persisted for several generations, while European farmer ancestry appears 223-515 years after the
introduction of Neolithic cultural practices in western Denmark and 324-474 years after in eastern
Denmark (68.3% probability). Dietary pa:erns also diﬀer signiﬁcantly by genetic ancestry after
accounting for chronology, while strontium isotope variation suggests possible diﬀerences in
landscape use between hunter-gatherer and farming communities. The introduction of Neolithic
practices, persistence of Mesolithic lifeways and population turnover therefore represent overlapping
rather than synchronous processes. More broadly, the study demonstrates how palaeogenomic
evidence can provide independent chronological information within Bayesian models, oﬀering a
transferable approach for disentangling cultural and population change by modelling these processes
separately and evaluating their temporal relationship.

Keywords: Mesolithic-Neolithic transition; neolithization; Bayesian chronological modelling;
palaeogenomics; genetic ancestry; Ertebølle Culture; Funnel Beaker Culture

\1. Introduction

Modern archaeology interprets the past through multiple sources and draws on a broad range
of disciplines. The archaeological material record remains at its heart, but methods from the natural
sciences have advanced the “biomolecular humanities” and greatly expanded our understanding of
past lifeways and societies (Oras, et al., 2025). The rapidly increasing palaeogenomic coverage and
mapping of genetic ancestry groups have reinvigorated earlier discussions of population migrations
based on distributions of archaeological evidence. At the same time, critics caution against simpliﬁed
migration narratives (Furholt, 2019, Hofmann, 2015) and call for closer integration of archaeological
and palaeogenomic evidence to understand the complex relationship between cultural and
demographic change (Bánﬀy and Whi:le, 2024, Eisenmann, et al., 2018). Recent studies have begun
addressing this challenge by integrating archaeological and palaeogenomic evidence within
demographic models (LaPolice, et al., 2025).
Palaeogenomics is still a relatively recent addition to the archaeological toolbox, and its full
potential has not yet been realised. Most studies have focused on population mobility across time
and space (e.g., Racimo, et al., 2020), and more recently on kinship practices and social organisation

cultural and population change independently, rather than assuming that the two processes were
necessarily synchronous.

1.1. The Mesolithic-Neolithic Transition

The Mesolithic-Neolithic transition occurred relatively late in Denmark around 4000-3950 BC,
and its rich and well-preserved archaeological record has received immense a:ention over the past
c. 175 years. The transition is deﬁned economically by a shift in subsistence strategy from ﬁshing,
hunting and gathering to pastoralism and agriculture, and culturally by the arrival of the Funnel
Beaker Culture, which replaced the Late Mesolithic Ertebølle Culture (Fischer and Kristiansen, 2002,
Sørensen, 2014, Sørensen, 2020).
The elements conventionally associated with the Neolithic appear to have emerged at diﬀerent
times. Growing evidence suggests that cereals and domesticated animals were introduced in multiple
steps, with ca:le and sheep/goat appearing earlier than pigs and cereals (Gron and Sørensen, 2018,
Sjögren, 2025). Dietary studies likewise indicate a marked shift from predominantly aquatic
Mesolithic diets towards more terrestrial Neolithic diets (Fischer, et al., 2007, Richards, et al., 2003),
although aquatic foods continued to be exploited (Lucquin, et al., 2023, McLaughlin, et al., 2025,
Milner, et al., 2004, Robson, et al., 2021). Together, these ﬁndings question the interpretation of the
Mesolithic-Neolithic transition as a single synchronous event around 4000 BC.

1.2. Migration, CULTURAL Transmission and Population Replacement

The relative importance of migration and cultural transmission in the spread of the Neolithic
remains one of the central debates in European prehistory. Traditional archaeological models have
interpreted the introduction of agriculture in Denmark either as the result of incoming farming
populations, the adoption of Neolithic practices by indigenous hunter-gatherers, or combinations of
the two (Fischer, 2002, Gron and Sørensen, 2018, Klassen, 2004, Larsson, 2007, Shennan, 2018,
Sørensen, 2020, Sørensen and Karg, 2014). Increasingly, however, the transition is viewed as a multi-
stranded process involving prolonged cultural, economic and demographic interactions between the
last hunter-gatherer communities and the ﬁrst farmers (Gron and Sørensen, 2018), while culturally
distinct communities may have coexisted within the same or neighbouring landscapes (Jensen and
Sørensen, 2023, Koivisto, et al., 2025).
Recent palaeogenomic research has transformed this discussion by demonstrating a rapid
population turnover at the onset of the Danish Neolithic, associated with the arrival of Early
European farmers (EF) carrying a mixture of Anatolian-derived farmer ancestry and non-local
Western Hunter-Gatherer (WHG) ancestry. Gene ﬂow from local Danish hunter-gatherers into
farming populations was initially limited, but increased later in the Neolithic (Allentoft, et al., 2024).
Genetic ancestry, however, need not coincide with cultural practices or identities. Archaeological and
palaeogenomic evidence therefore provide complementary rather than interchangeable perspectives
on the Mesolithic-Neolithic transition, highlighting the need for chronological frameworks capable
of evaluating cultural and population change independently.

1.3. Chronological Frameworks

Inﬂuential studies by Fischer et al. (2007) and Sørensen & Karg (2014) present large radiocarbon
(14C) datasets supporting a Mesolithic-Neolithic transformation around 4000/3950 BC in Southern
Scandinavia. Establishing the temporal relationship between the diﬀerent cultural and population
processes involved in this transition, however, requires more than identifying a single chronological
boundary.

Evaluating the timing of these processes is not straightforward. Visual inspection of calibrated
14C dates can exaggerate date ranges and fails to account for multimodal probability distributions or
the non-independence of related dates (Bayliss, 2015). Bayesian statistics instead provides a
framework for integrating 14C evidence with prior information to estimate the timing of events that
cannot be dated directly and to evaluate competing archaeological scenarios (Bayliss, 2007, Buck, et
al., 1996). The choice of prior information is critical to the resulting chronological models. Bayesian
chronological studies increasingly integrate multiple forms of archaeological evidence as priors (e.g.,
Griﬃths, et al., 2022, Rose and Meadows, 2024). Palaeogenomic data raise the possibility that genetic
ancestry groups, and in some cases genetic kinships, may likewise provide chronological
information. Such applications remain few, but Allentoft et al. (2024) use genetic ancestry data to
estimate a rapid Mesolithic-Neolithic transition across Denmark beginning around 3900 BC. Their
model assigns individuals with WHG and EF ancestry to successive phases separated by a
trapezoidal transition, thereby preventing possible temporal overlap between the two ancestry
groups from being investigated. Whether cultural and genetic transitions were synchronous and the
extent to which palaeogenomic evidence can provide independent chronological information
therefore remain to be tested formally.
This study investigates the Late Mesolithic-Early Neolithic transition in Denmark by modelling
cultural and genetic transitions separately within a Bayesian framework, formally testing their
synchronicity while evaluating the chronological potential of palaeogenomic evidence.

\2. Material and Methods

2.1. Data Selection

A dataset focused on the Late Mesolithic-Early Neolithic in present-day Denmark is compiled
through an extensive literature review. The dataset integrates archaeological, chronological, isotopic,
and palaeogenomic information, including site context and cultural association, 14C ages, dietary
(δ13C, δ15N) and strontium (Sr87/Sr86) isotope measurements, genetic ancestry, and sample material
(palaeobotanical, zooarchaeological and human skeletal remains). Sites and ﬁnd contexts are
included if they contain at least one 14C date associated with either a cultural a:ribution or a genetic
ancestry assignment, allowing wetland deposits lacking archaeological context to be included where
genetic ancestry information is available. 14C dates on marine and freshwater organisms, food crusts,
fa:y residues and peat are excluded because of their potentially large reservoir oﬀsets.
The ﬁnal dataset comprises 494 14C dates from 130 archaeological sites (western Denmark = 56,
eastern Denmark = 74, Fig. 1a-b). Of these, 143 14C dates are from human remains, of which 108 also
have dietary stable isotope measurements determined by IRMS. Strontium isotope ratios (87Sr/86Sr)
are available for 52 individuals and genetic ancestry information for 67 individuals (WHG = 31, EF =
34, Baltic hunter-gatherer (BHG) = 2). Analytical methods vary according to data type and laboratory,
and methodological details are provided in the original publications (Appendix A).
Data coverage is particularly good in eastern and central Denmark but comparatively sparse in
western and southern Jutland (Fig. 1a), reﬂecting taphonomic biases that favour collagen
preservation in eastern Denmark. Previous isotopic and palaeogenomic studies have therefore also
focussed on eastern Denmark (Allentoft, et al., 2024, Fischer, et al., 2007), and the available isotopic
and palaeogenomic evidence consequently remains geographically uneven.

2.2. Dietary Reconstruction and Reservoir Oﬀsets

Consumption of aquatic foods (marine and freshwater) causes 14C age oﬀsets because aquatic
organisms are depleted in 14C relative to atmospheric CO2 (Heaton, et al., 2020). Humans, dogs and
pigs may consume a mixture of terrestrial and aquatic foods, resulting in mixed carbon signals that
require 14C age correction (Stuiver, et al., 1986). The proportions of marine and freshwater protein in
individual diets are reconstructed using the FRUITS Bayesian multi-proxy mixing model, which
estimates the contribution of selected food groups to the consumer’s diet based on IRMS
measurements of dietary stable isotope values (δ13C, δ15N) (Fernandes, et al., 2014). Full details of the
approach and estimated dietary reservoir oﬀsets are provided in Appendix A and B.1.
FRUITS dietary estimates are additionally used to examine dietary variation by genetic ancestry
and modelled date. Associations between marine dietary contribution, modelled median date and
genetic ancestry are evaluated using multiple linear regression, restricted to individuals with WHG
and EF ancestry because of the small BHG sample. The independent contribution of each predictor is
evaluated by single-term deletion using F-tests. Freshwater contribution is assessed descriptively
because of the comparatively large uncertainties associated with the FRUITS estimates.

2.3. Bayesian Chronological Modelling

Bayesian chronological models are constructed in OxCal v4.4 (Bronk Ramsey, 2009a) using the
Northern Hemisphere atmospheric calibration curve IntCal20 (Reimer, et al., 2020). The exact OxCal
CQL code is provided in Appendix E.
Sites are modelled individually and incorporate prior information on cultural association,
stratigraphy and sample material (Appendix S4). No explicit kinship constraints are applied because
those genetically related individuals in the dataset are interpreted as broadly contemporaneous and
therefore do not provide additional chronological information. 14C ages with dietary reservoir oﬀsets
are modelled using the Delta_R function in OxCal (Bronk Ramsey, 2009a) with minimum and
maximum oﬀsets estimated using the relevant regional marine and freshwater reservoir ages
(Appendix B.1). 14C ages with suspected dietary reservoir oﬀsets, but lacking stable isotope
measurements, are modelled as terminus post quem or marked as outliers. A default charcoal outlier
model is added to all samples of charcoal, charred wood and wood with potentially substantial
intrinsic ages (Bronk Ramsey, 2009b), and obvious residual or intrusive samples are marked as
outliers. Relevant posterior boundary estimates from individual site models are cross-referenced to
a cultural transition model estimating the timing of the Mesolithic-Neolithic transformation and to
an ancestry transition model estimating the end of the WHG ancestry phase and the beginning of the
EF ancestry phase (Fig. 2). Estimated end boundaries of Ertebølle sites contribute to the Mesolithic
phase of the cultural transition model, whereas estimated start boundaries of Funnel Beaker sites
contribute to the Neolithic phase. Likewise, individuals with WHG or EF ancestry are cross-
referenced to their respective ancestry phases and, where archaeological context permits, also to the
cultural transition model. Individuals lacking contextual information are therefore constrained solely

2.4. Strontium Isotope Methods.

Measured 87Sr/86Sr values and genetic data are compiled from Allentoft et al. (2024), Sjögren &
Fischer (2023), Fischer et al. (2007), Maring & Riede (2019) and Price et al. (2007) and are re-interpreted

variation within the dataset, observations are grouped using the Jenks Natural Breaks optimization
method (Jenks, 1967, Jenks and Caspall, 1971). Associations between genetic ancestry and strontium
class are evaluated using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test.

\3. Results

3.1. Modelled Chronologies

Model A estimates a single Mesolithic-Neolithic cultural transition and ancestry transition
across Denmark, assuming that the transitions occurred contemporaneously across the region. The
Mesolithic and Neolithic are modelled as overlapping bounded phases, allowing the Neolithic to start
before the Mesolithic ends. The model is not accepted (Amodel = 44.9), suggesting regional diﬀerences
in the timing of the cultural transition.
Model B estimates the Mesolithic-Neolithic cultural transition and ancestry transition separately
for western and eastern Denmark. In Model B1, the respective transitions are modelled using
trapezoidal boundary parameters, assuming sequential phases (Lee and Bronk Ramsey, 2012); the
model otherwise follows Model A. Both regional models are accepted, and principal posterior
estimates are summarised in Table 1 and Figure 3. Model B1 estimates short cultural transitions in
both regions, while the temporal relationship between WHG and EF ancestry diﬀers. In western
Denmark, the WHG phase ends before the EF phase begins, whereas in eastern Denmark, the WHG
phase ends after the beginning of the EF phase. In eastern Denmark, the posterior distribution for the
start of EF ancestry is multimodal, although relatively well constrained compared with the estimated
end of WHG ancestry.

3.2. Chronological Sensitivity Testing

The robustness of the preferred Model B2 is assessed through a series of alternative modelling
scenarios. Models A and B1 already provide sensitivity tests of the key modelling assumptions by
evaluating, respectively, a single cultural transition across Denmark and separate regional
Mesolithic-Neolithic transitions that may extend over time but do not permit overlap between the
two phases. To further assess the contribution of ancestry-based constraints, Model C retains the
structure of Model B2 but excludes all ancestry information. Model C produces posterior probability
distributions closely comparable to those of the preferred Model B2, indicating that removing the
ancestry-based constraints does not substantially alter the estimated cultural chronology. Genetic
ancestry therefore provides li:le additional chronological resolution in this dataset, which is already
well constrained by radiocarbon and archaeological evidence.

3.3. Dietary Variation by Genetic Ancestry and Modelled Date

Dietary and genetic ancestry information is available for 63 individuals. FRUITS estimates a
mean contribution of 28.7% (range 5.2-66.1%) from marine ﬁsh and mammals and 22.8% (range 6.4-
47.8%) from freshwater ﬁsh (Appendix A). Dietary composition shows clear variation through time
and between genetic ancestry groups (Fig. 5). WHG individuals dating to the Mesolithic show higher
marine contributions, whereas EF and BHG individuals dating to the Neolithic show lower marine
and higher freshwater contributions. Some overlap between ancestry groups is nevertheless
apparent, particularly around and after the Mesolithic-Neolithic transition. A multiple linear
regression model including modelled median date and genetic ancestry (WHG and EF) as predictors
of marine dietary contribution indicates that ancestry is signiﬁcantly associated with marine
contribution after accounting for date (F = 29.86, p \< 0.001), whereas date is not signiﬁcantly associated
with marine contribution after accounting for ancestry (F = 3.36, p = 0.072). This suggests that the
observed diﬀerences in marine dietary contribution are more strongly associated with genetic
ancestry than with chronology alone. The small number of BHG individuals prevents their inclusion
in the statistical comparison. Freshwater contribution is not subjected to statistical testing because the
comparatively large uncertainties associated with the FRUITS estimates limit meaningful comparison
between groups.

\4. Discussion

4.1. The Mesolithic-Neolithic Transition in Denmark

The Mesolithic-Neolithic transition varied considerably in pace and timing across Europe
(Bocquet-Appel, et al., 2012, Shennan, 2018). Reconstructing this process across present-day Denmark
is challenging because of regional diﬀerences in archaeological visibility and preservation.
Nevertheless, the present dataset supports a broadly contemporaneous onset of Neolithic cultural
activity across Denmark around 4100-3950 BC, consistent with previous research (Fischer and
Kristiansen, 2002, Sørensen, 2014, Sørensen, 2020). The chronology places this onset earlier than in
southern Norway (around 3900 BC), broadly contemporaneous with southern Sweden (around 4000
BC), and later than along the Baltic coast of northern Germany (around 4100 BC) (Andersson, et al.,
2016, HarY, et al., 2007, Nielsen, 2021, Solheim, 2021, Sørensen, 2014). Mesolithic lifeways, however,
persisted until around 3950-3750 BC. This period of cultural overlap is substantially shorter than
suggested by recent demographic modelling based on summed radiocarbon probability
distributions, which inferred prolonged hunter-gatherer persistence in Denmark (Cortell-Nicolau, et
al., 2025). The diﬀerence likely reﬂects the contrasting modelling approaches: Cortell-Nicolau et al.
use radiocarbon-date frequencies as proxies for population dynamics, whereas the present study
estimates the chronological boundaries of archaeologically deﬁned cultural activity. The estimated
persistence of Mesolithic lifeways is also consistent with recent evidence for sustained, and in some
areas intensiﬁed, coastal resource exploitation during and beyond the transition (McLaughlin, et al.,
2025). Together, these results suggest that the Danish transition was characterised by the rapid
establishment of Neolithic cultural practices alongside a more gradual abandonment of Mesolithic
lifeways.

4.2. Late Surviving Hunter-Gatherers

The palaeogenomic evidence indicates that local Danish hunter-gatherers made only a limited
contribution to the earliest Neolithic gene pool, with gene ﬂow between late surviving hunter-
gatherers and farming communities increasing later in the Neolithic (Allentoft, et al., 2024). A similar
pa:ern has been observed in Central Europe, where genetic exchange increased gradually over
successive generations during the Neolithic expansion (Tsoupas, et al., 2025, Vondrovský, 2026).
Nevertheless, this does not exclude the coexistence of Mesolithic and Neolithic communities or the
adoption of cultural identities diﬀerent from those suggested by genetic ancestry.
The most prominent example of the la:er is the Dragsholm man, a young man around 20 years
at death with WHG ancestry, who was buried with characteristic grave goods from the Funnel Beaker
Culture (Price, et al., 2007). In total, three individuals with WHG ancestry are currently known from
the Neolithic period, together with two individuals dating to the Mesolithic-Neolithic transition, all
from eastern Denmark. Dietary information is available for four of these individuals, providing
further insight into their lifeways (Fig. 7). Despite their WHG ancestry, the Dragsholm man,
Ravnsbjerggård II and Elkenøre have relatively low marine dietary contributions, resembling the
general pa:ern among EF individuals. Rødhals, in contrast, retains a substantially higher marine
contribution, more characteristic of earlier WHG individuals (cf. Fig. 5). The two individuals with

4.3. Migrating Farmers

The chronology established here raises the question of how Neolithic practices were introduced
into Denmark. Late Mesolithic communities of Southern Scandinavia maintained contacts with
Neolithic societies on the continent, as demonstrated by imported artefacts including shoe-last axes,
jadeite and ﬂat copper axes (Klassen, 2000, Klassen, 2002, Klassen, 2004). Early cereals and
domesticated animals may likewise reﬂect such interactions, possibly representing scouting
expeditions or small-scale pioneer se:lements before 4000 BC, followed by larger-scale migration and
the establishment of a fully Neolithic economy (Gron and Sørensen, 2018, Sjögren, 2025, Sørensen,
2020).
Neither the palaeogenomic nor the strontium isotope evidence allows the pioneer phase of
Neolithic migration into Denmark to be identiﬁed directly. As none of the individuals carrying EF
ancestry can be identiﬁed as ﬁrst-generation migrants, they provide only a terminus ante quem for the
introduction of this ancestry group. This limitation is partly accommodated by the Bayesian
chronological model, which assumes that the earliest occurrences of a phenomenon are unlikely to
be sampled and dated, although whether this adequately accounts for the absence of the earliest
generations remains uncertain. The isotope data suggest diﬀerences between ancestry groups, with
WHG individuals concentrated in the lower strontium classes and EF individuals more broadly
distributed across the lower and intermediate classes. However, small sample sizes and similar
strontium baseline values across large parts of Western Europe limit interpretation of these
diﬀerences. More broadly, taphonomic and sampling biases may limit the archaeological and

4.4. Modelling Cultural and Genetic Change

The earliest appearance of the Funnel Beaker Culture in Denmark has been interpreted as
marking the arrival of Early European farming populations (Allentoft, et al., 2024). By modelling the
cultural and genetic transitions independently, however, the present study provides a formal
chronological test of this interpretation. Posterior diﬀerence estimates indicate that EF ancestry
appeared 223-515 years after the introduction of Neolithic cultural practices in western Denmark and
324-474 years after their introduction in eastern Denmark (68.3% probability). Mesolithic practices
continued for 118-330 years after the introduction of the Neolithic in western Denmark and 70-269
years after its introduction in eastern Denmark (68.3% probability). Considered together, the
continued Mesolithic cultural activity and the presence of late-surviving individuals with hunter-
gatherer ancestry are consistent with small hunter-gatherer groups persisting alongside farming
communities for several generations after the introduction of the Neolithic. These results demonstrate
that cultural change and population turnover unfolded on diﬀerent timescales, with the introduction
of Neolithic cultural practices, the persistence of Mesolithic lifeways, and the expansion of
populations carrying EF ancestry representing overlapping rather than synchronous processes.
The observed temporal oﬀsets may have resulted from several processes. Neolithic practices
may initially have spread through cultural interactions, although the practical knowledge required
for agriculture and the rapid rate of Neolithic expansion have been argued to favour substantial
demic diﬀusion (Fort, et al., 2018, Sørensen, 2020). Alternatively, the earliest communities carrying
EF ancestry may have remained demographically small and genetically underrepresented in the
current dataset because of preservation and sampling biases despite producing an archaeologically
visible cultural record. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and the apparent temporal
oﬀset need not reﬂect a simple delay in the arrival of EF ancestry.
The resulting pa:ern is consistent with a period of cultural duality or ‘inner peripheries’ (Jensen
and Sørensen, 2023, Vondrovský, 2026), in which hunter-gatherers and incoming farmers maintained
distinct ways of life within the same or neighbouring landscapes. Similar pa:erns are documented in
Central Europe (Gehlen, 2017, Stäuble, et al., 2021). Modelling of archaeological and palaeogenomic
evidence from the European Neolithic expansion suggests that such cultural distinctions may have
been maintained through limited cultural transmission between farmer and hunter-gatherer
populations and predominantly within-group mating (LaPolice, et al., 2025). In Denmark, continued
use of kitchen middens and sustained exploitation of marine resources provide further evidence for
the persistence of distinct hunter-gatherer lifeways during the transition (McLaughlin, et al., 2025).
The dietary evidence likewise indicates diﬀerences between ancestry groups, with WHG ancestry
signiﬁcantly associated with higher marine dietary contributions after accounting for chronology.
The late WHG individuals discussed above, however, demonstrate that this relationship was not
absolute. The strontium data may likewise be consistent with diﬀerentiated landscape use between
ancestry groups, although the observed diﬀerences cannot currently be translated directly into
geographical origins. For example, at Syltholm Fjord, ﬁshing practices continued from the Mesolithic
into the Neolithic and beyond (Fig. C.3.2) (Koivisto, et al., 2025). Hunter-gatherer activities may have
remained focused on coastal environments while farming communities established se:lements
further inland on suitable agricultural soils (Gron and Sørensen, 2018, Sørensen, 2016), a pa:ern
potentially consistent with the strontium evidence. As illustrated by the Dragsholm man, however,
genetic ancestry and cultural aﬃliation were not necessarily coupled.
Taken together, the archaeological, chronological, isotopic and palaeogenomic evidence
supports a three-stage model of the Danish Mesolithic-Neolithic transition, broadly comparable to
that proposed by Gron & Sørensen (2018). An interaction phase before 4000 BC involved contacts
between hunter-gatherers and farming communities, reﬂected in imported artefacts, early

4.5. The Chronological Potential of Palaeogenomic Evidence

Palaeogenomic evidence has become increasingly available since around 2015 as advances in
next-generation sequencing made large-scale ancient DNA studies feasible, but its inherent
chronological potential has yet to be widely exploited. This study demonstrates that palaeogenomic
data can be treated as a source of chronological evidence, rather than solely as evidence for ancestry,
migration, or kinship. Just as archaeological typologies have long been incorporated as prior
information within Bayesian chronological models, genetic ancestry groups can likewise provide
prior information. Genetic kinships can also constrain the temporal separation between related
individuals (e.g., Meadows, et al., 2020), providing an additional source of chronological evidence.
Sensitivity testing shows that removing the ancestry-based constraints has li:le inﬂuence on the
posterior estimates in the present study. This likely reﬂects the well-documented 14C and
archaeological dataset available for the Danish Mesolithic-Neolithic transition, which already
provides strong chronological constraints. In less well-constrained chronological contexts, or where
genetic data provide independent chronological structure not captured by archaeological priors, the
contribution of palaeogenomic evidence may be considerably greater. As palaeogenomic datasets
continue to expand, recognising their chronological value could potentially improve archaeological
chronologies across a wide range of contexts.

\5. Conclusion

The archaeological record is a ﬁnite resource with the ethical implication that we must always
aim to maximize the knowledge gained before carrying out destructive sampling (Källén, et al., 2024).
The present study demonstrates how legacy data can be reanalysed using modern analytical
approaches to generate new archaeological insights. By modelling cultural and genetic transitions
independently within a Bayesian framework, this approach allows their temporal relationship to be
evaluated explicitly and quantitatively. Taken together, the chronological, palaeogenomic and
isotopic evidence show that the Danish Mesolithic-Neolithic transition was not a single synchronous
event but a series of overlapping cultural and demographic processes. The rapid establishment of
Neolithic cultural practices around 4000 BC was accompanied by persistent Mesolithic lifeways,
while substantial population turnover unfolded over the following centuries as European farmer
ancestry became widespread.
Beyond the Danish case, this study illustrates how palaeogenomic evidence can be incorporated
as an independent source of chronological information within Bayesian models. Genetic ancestry
contributed only limited additional chronological resolution in the Danish dataset, likely because the
chronology is already well constrained by archaeological and 14C evidence. The contribution of
palaeogenomic evidence may be greater in contexts where existing chronological constraints are
weaker. More broadly, modelling cultural and genetic evidence independently provides a framework
for testing the temporal relationship between cultural and population change in other prehistoric
transitions.
