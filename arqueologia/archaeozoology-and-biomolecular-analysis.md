Abstract

Faunal remains constitute one of the main sources of evidence for reconstructing foodways
and economic practices in Phoenician societies. Archaeological research conducted over
recent decades in the Iberian Peninsula has highlighted their importance for understanding
food production processes, maintenance activities, and animal consumption patterns linked
to livestock management, ﬁshing, shellﬁsh gathering, and the use of bone for tool making.
In this paper, we present the results of zooarchaeological and taphonomic analyses of
mammal, bird, ﬁsh, and mollusc remains recovered from the archaeological excavation
at the corner of Sagasta and Tinte Streets in Cádiz, Spain. These data are integrated with
biomolecular analyses, speciﬁcally ancient DNA of bovine specimens, and organic residue
analysis of two storage vessels. Radiocarbon dating places these remains at the end of
the 9th and the beginning of the 8th century BC, providing a key reference point for
understanding archaic Phoenician occupation in the region. The archaeofaunal evidence
highlights the central role of livestock, particularly sheep, goats and cattle, alongside the
intensive use of marine resources such as ﬁsh and molluscs, and the exploitation of wetland
environments for duck hunting. The archaeogenomics analysis of two bovine remains

\1. Introduction
Foodway in the Phoenician world is a subject that has already been studied by sev-
eral researchers in the Mediterranean area of the Iberian Peninsula (e.g., Delgado, 2008;
Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas, 2011, 2020; Pardo Barrionuevo, 2015; Marín-Aguilera,
2016; Pérez-Jordà, 2020; Valenzuela-Lamas, 2020). From a historiographical point of view,
faunal remains have been the most common element used in the archaeological record
to understand the essential aspects of the food and culinary practices of the Phoenicians,
such as the consumption of livestock and its by-products (e.g., Estévez, 1983; Morales et al.,
1994; Riquelme, 2001; Estaca et al., 2015; Iborra & Vives-Ferrándiz, 2015; Valenzuela-Lamas,
2020), ﬁshing and ﬁsh canning (Roselló & Morales, 1994; García Vargas, 2001; Morales &
Roselló, 2012; Moya Cobos, 2016; Marlasca, 2020), mollusc harvesting (Roselló & Morales,
1990; Moreno, 1994; Oller et al., 1999) or poultry consumption and trade (Hernández &
Jonsson, 1994; Castaños, 1994; Albizuri et al., 2020).
These studies have recently been joined by biomolecular analyses of ancient DNA
from Phoenician populations in the Iberian Peninsula, offering new results on the genetic
origin of Phoenician settlers in the Iberian Peninsula and their importance in later periods
(e.g., P. A. Zalloua et al., 2008; P. Zalloua et al., 2018; Gomes et al., 2023; Ringbauer et al.,
2025). In contrast, genetic studies focusing on domestic animals remain scarce (Ginja &
Bruno de Sousa, 2024). As a result, important questions concerning the origins of livestock
introduced by Phoenician communities, their possible adaptation to local environments
and their role within colonial economic systems remain poorly understood.
Within biomolecular studies, the analysis of organic residues in Phoenician and Punic
ceramic contents has increasingly become a common practice in research over the past few
decades (e.g., Peche-Quilichini et al., 2017; Bison, 2020; Bison et al., 2020; Bonetto et al.,
2020). These analyses have made it possible to determine aspects related to the culinary
use of ceramic vessels (e.g., Bison, 2020; Bison et al., 2020), the transport of certain products
(e.g., Bonetto et al., 2020) or even the use of natural adhesives to repair ceramics (e.g., Peche-
Quilichini et al., 2017). However, signiﬁcant questions are still unanswered regarding the
relationship between the contents identiﬁed in pottery and the faunal remains documented
at settlements, particularly concerning the types of animal products consumed, processed,
stored or transported by Phoenician communities.
However, despite all this progress, several aspects of Phoenician foodways remain
insufﬁciently understood. While previous studies have demonstrated the importance
of livestock, ﬁshing and shellﬁsh gathering across Phoenician settlements, the relative
contribution of terrestrial and marine resources, the extent to which animals were exploited
for meat or secondary products, and the relationship between animal consumption and
the storage or transport of foodstuffs in ceramic containers have yet to be resolved. These
issues are particularly relevant for the earliest phases of Gadir, where archaeozoological
and biomolecular evidence is limited.
The main aim of this paper is to reconstruct the foodways and economic practices
of the earliest Phoenician settlers in Gadir through the integration of archaeozoological,
taphonomic and biomolecular evidence. To achieve this objective, we analyse faunal
remains recovered from the Sagasta–Tinte site using archaeozoological and taphonomic
approaches, complemented by ancient DNA analysis of two bovine remains and organic
residue analysis from two transport vessels associated with a Phoenician occupation layer
dated to the end of the 9th and the beginning of the 8th century BC. The integration of these
analytical approaches provides new insights into animal exploitation, food processing,
consumption practices, and the storage or transport of food products during the archaic
Phoenician period.

\2. Sagasta–Tinte Site (Andalusia, Spain)
The Sagasta–Tinte site is located in the old town of the modern city of Cádiz (Andalu-
sia, Spain), speciﬁcally at the intersection of Sagasta and Tinte streets. It represents the
northernmost point within the city where remains associated with the ancient Phoenician
colony of Gadir have been documented to date (Figure 1A).

Archaeological ﬁndings in the Cádiz suggest that the Phoenician foundation of Gadir
was built towards the end of the 9th century BC (Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas, 2020;
Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas et al., in press-a). This is the case of the Teatro Cómico,
which witnesses a sequence spanning from the 9th century BC until its deﬁnitive destruction
in the 6th century BC (Torres et al., 2020). Other areas of early Phoenician occupation have
also been identiﬁed around this enclave, mainly between Cánovas del Castillo and Ancha
streets, with occupations between the end of the 9th to 8th century BC (Córdoba & Ruiz
Mata, 2005; Ruiz Mata et al., 2014, 2020). In this context, the remains found at Sagasta–Tinte
would have formed part of the original Phoenician foundation of Gadir, which we know
about through Strabo (III, 5, 5).
The archaeological records associated with this context are mostly pottery, mainly
wheel-made, and similar to those recovered from other archaic sites in Cádiz (Niveau-de-
Villedary y Mariñas et al., 2024, in press-a). This includes storage and transport vessels, both
of far western and central Mediterranean (Sardinian origin) as well as eastern manufacture,
carinated bowls, thin-lipped plates, and oil lamps. In addition, high-quality ceramics
brought from the metropolis appear, along with the typical Tyrian bowl-plates and others
with bichrome decoration. Among the closed shapes dipper juglets, oil bottles, stilted rim
jars and neck ridge jugs were identiﬁed, both with and without external treatment. Most of
the storage and processing ceramics are handmade and of local tradition. Among these
elements we must highlight a large “à chardon” vessel that appeared almost entirely in
situ, together with burnished ceramics with reticulate decoration, ceramics with sgrafﬁto
decoration, and other locally handmade forms (Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas et al., in
press-a). The ceramic repertoire is completed by a series of handmade Sardinian imports
(“a collo” and biconical vessel). From a chrono-typological point of view, these ceramics
belong the ﬁnal moments of the “Salamis Horizon” and transition to the “Kition Horizon”
of Cypris, levels IV-III of Tyre and period III-IV of Al-Bass (Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas
et al., in press-a), moments for which we proposed dates ca. 780 BC (Niveau-de-Villedary y
Mariñas, 2020, p. 339), in conventional absolute dates. Sequentially, it is included in the
“Early Phoenician Colonial Horizon” deﬁned based on the ﬁndings of Huelva/Méndez
Núñez-Plaza de las Monjas, Málaga/La Rebanadilla, El Carambolo or Útica (López Castro,
\2021) and would belong to the ﬁnal stages of the Levantine Late Iron Age A—transition to
the Late Iron Age B (Núñez Calvo, 2014).
The discovery of a small foundational deposit under one of the better-preserved
foundations (SU29) has provided, for the ﬁrst time in Gadir, a series of three radiocarbon
dates that can date the urbanisation of the site and differentiate the brief 40–50 year
period between the settlement’s construction (around 800 BC) and its last occupation
(ca. 760–750 BC) (Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas et al., 2024, in press-a) (Table 1). This
ﬁrst Phoenician horizon identiﬁed at Sagasta–Tinte (corresponding to Phase 1B of Teatro
Cómico) must be placed towards the end of the Levantine Late Iron A (Núñez Calvo,
2008), stratum V (?)/IV of Tyre, Phase II of Al-Bass, the end of the “Salamis Horizon” in
Cyprus (Torres et al., 2020), and the beginning of the ﬁrst western wheel-made production
phases (horizons M1/At1) (Ramón, 2010)—whose upper limit must be extended by a few
decades. This places Sagasta–Tinte in Phase II of the early Phoenician Settlements (López

In addition to the ceramic remains at Sagasta–Tinte, a set of 12 charcoal samples
have been recovered, identifying four different taxa, highlighting the use of lentisk wood
(Pistacia sp.), followed by heath (Erica sp.), pine (Pinus halepensis) and plum tree wood
(Prunus sp.). Some samples could not be identiﬁed further than belonging to Angiosperms
due to their poor preservation. The species used are similar to those removed from some
cremation necropolis from the area of Gadir (Carrión et al., 2024) and offer an image
of the surrounding environment between the 8th and 6th century BC, which, though
partial, contributes relevant information regarding the exploitation of natural resources and
the environmental conditions which favoured it (Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas et al., 2024,
in press-a).
One of the most signiﬁcant ﬁnds from the excavation was the discovery of remains
associated with various metallurgical activities carried out in this area of ancient Gadir.
These remains include a range of by-products (such as prills and slag), as well as ceramic
elements linked to metal production (crucibles and nozzles), ores, and fragments belonging
to at least one smelting furnace. These metallurgical elements were recovered from different
levels dated to the 8th century BC, corresponding to the site’s Phoenician archaic phase.
The metallurgical remains appear to be primarily associated with copper-based production.
However, given the archaeological context, it is also possible that some of these materials
were stored as scrap for later remelting. Through a combination of SEM-EDS analysis of
11 samples (4 crucibles, 3 nozzles, 2 slags, and 1 adobe fragment) and XRD analysis of
22 samples, all from the archaic Phoenician phase of Gadir, four distinct types of metallurgi-
cal production have been identiﬁed within the workshop: copper, tin, bronze, and leaded
bronze (Bashore et al., 2024; Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas et al., in press-a).
Finally, the faunal remains analysed in this paper originate from various stratigraphic
units of the site, all associated with different phases of Phoenician occupation. The assem-
blage includes mammals, ﬁsh, birds, and molluscs.

\3. Materials and Methods
All the remains recovered during the excavation were analysed from both archaeo-
zoological and taphonomic perspectives. In addition to hand collection, the sediment
was sieved using meshes of up to 2 mm. Although the use of ﬁner mesh sizes may have
increased the recovery of the smallest faunal remains, particularly ﬁshes, birds and small
mammals, the assemblage provides sufﬁcient evidence to investigate the main patterns of
animal exploitation documented at the site.

In the case of mammals, all remains were classiﬁed anatomically and taxonomically
by stratigraphic unit, using various osteological atlases (Barone, 1976; Schmid, 1972; Pales
& García, 1981; Varela & Rodríguez, 2004) and osteological reference collections of the
International Institute for Prehistoric Research of Cantabria (University of Cantabria) and
the Archaeozoology lab of the University of Salamanca. When certain remains could not be
identiﬁed to species or family level, they were grouped into three size categories based on
cortical bone thickness and robustness: large-sized (>300 kg), medium-sized (100–300 kg),
and small-sized mammals (\<100 kg). For molluscs, taxonomic identiﬁcation was based on
external shell characteristics (sculpture, colouration, and morphology), and nomenclature
was veriﬁed using the World Register of Marine Species (https://www.marinespecies.org/)
and specialised literature (Sabelli, 1980; Lindner, 1983; Gofas et al., 2011; Hayward et al.,
1998). For the ichthyofauna, identiﬁcation was carried out through comparative anatomy
and the reference osteological collection of R. Marlasca. Bird remains were identiﬁed based
on morphological criteria and with the help of osteological collections, as well as specialised
atlases of birds (Woelﬂe, 1967; Cohen & Serjeantson, 1996; Bochenski & Tomek, 2009).
For skeletal proﬁle studies, remains were classiﬁed into anatomical regions: cranial
(antler, skull, maxilla, mandible), axial (vertebra, rib, sternum, scapula, pelvis, sacrum, ﬂat
bone), forelimb (humerus, radius, ulna, carpal, metacarpal), and hindlimb (femur, patella,
tibia, ﬁbula, tarsal, metatarsal). Indeterminate appendicular bones were categorised as
limbs (metapodial, phalanx, sesamoid, long bone shaft, epiphysis) (Yravedra & Domínguez-
Rodrigo, 2009).
For taphonomic analysis, bone surfaces were examined with hand magnifying glasses
(10× and 15×) and the microscope Leica EZ4 HD (6.5–32×). Among the anthropogenic
modifications identified, cut marks associated with skinning, evisceration, disarticulation,
dismemberment, and defleshing were recorded based on their morphology, location, and
orientation on the bone (Binford, 1981; Pérez Ripoll, 1992; Galán & Domínguez-Rodrigo, 2013).
Thermal alterations were also documented, indicating varying degrees of exposure to fire,
inferred from the colouration of the bone cortex (Stiner et al., 1995). Carnivore modifications
were classified according to their morphology, noting their position and orientation on the
bone (Selvaggio, 1994; Stiner, 1994). Additionally, alterations from the formation of the
archaeological deposit were considered, including plant roots, weathering, manganese oxide
precipitation, and post-depositional processes (Fernández-Jalvo & Andrews, 2016).
For quantifying the archaeofaunal record, the Number of Remains (NR), Number of
Identiﬁed Specimens (NISP), and Minimum Number of Individuals (MNI) were calculated
(Lyman, 1994). Age of death was determined by examining epiphyseal fusion (Barone,
1976; Silver, 1963), and dental eruption and wear patterns (Payne, 1973; Pérez Ripoll,
1988; Hillson, 2005; Greenﬁeld & Arnold, 2008). Individuals were classiﬁed into four age
categories: neonate, immature, juvenile and adult.
A prospective ancient DNA analysis was carried out on two bovine remains from
SU29 and SU71. Sub-sampling of these bone specimens and genomic analyses were carried
out by the BIOPOLIS-CIBIO-InBio team of the University of Porto in dedicated facilities.
Samples were subjected to a prewash bleach treatment and DNA extraction combined
optimised methods from Dabney and Meyer (2019) and Boessenkool et al. (2017). Double-
stranded Illumina shotgun sequencing libraries were prepared according to Meyer and
Kircher (2010). Paired-end (100 bp) libraries were screened on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000
instrument (Trinity Genome Sequencing Laboratory, Trinity College Dublin, Ireland). The
quality of the raw sequencing data was assessed using the FastQC v.0.11.9 software (2010)
and sex determination followed the method described by Flamingh et al. (2020). In addition,
samples were taken for isotopic analysis, pre-treatment was carried out at the University of
Tübingen, and the results are still pending.

Finally, to determine the organic content of ceramic remains from the site, organic residue
analysis was carried out in two storage vessels. Samples were taken from the internal surface
using a modelling drill. An initial layer was removed to eliminate potential surface contam-
ination, followed by a second layer for analysis. A standard lipid extraction protocol was
followed (Mottram et al., 1999), with the addition of an internal standard to each sample (20 µL
of a solution of C34 n-alkane at 1 mg/mL) and the use of a dichloromethane and methanol
mixture (DCM:MeOH; 2:1 v/v). Subsequently, a protocol for aldaric acid extraction was
applied using a mixture of boron trifluoride, butan-1-ol, and cyclohexane (Garnier & Valamoti,
2016). The samples were trimethylsilylated using N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide
(BSTFA, 40 µL) and pyridine (4 µL) as a catalyst, and analysed by Gas Chromatography (GC)
and GC–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) using an Agilent Technologies 5977A MSD (Agilent
Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and FID system. Further details on the GC-MS analyses
can be found in the Methodology Section of Rageot et al. (2019).

\4. Results
A total of 321 faunal remains were documented at the Sagasta–Tinte site. Of these,
158 correspond to mammals (49.22%), 3 to birds (0.93%), 12 to ﬁsh (3.74%), and 148 to
molluscs (46.11%).

4.1. Mammals
Of the total remains, only 82 could be identiﬁed at the species or family level. The
remaining were classiﬁed by size category, comprising large-sized animals (NR = 22),
medium-sized animals (NR = 2), and small-sized animals (NR = 43). In addition, 9 frag-
ments were too indeterminate to classify. Among the identiﬁable remains, caprines are the
most abundant (NR = 67), including sheep (NISP = 18) and goats (NISP = 9). These are
followed by cattle, with 13 remains, and dog, with 2 remains (Table 2).
Regarding MNI, two goats were identiﬁed: one adult and one juvenile under two years
of age. Sheep are represented by three individuals: two adults and one juvenile, whose
age could not be further speciﬁed. Among the remains classiﬁed as Caprinae, there is one
neonate represented by a pelvic fragment, one immature aged between 4 and 8 months,
one juvenile under two years, and two adults. Cattle are represented by two individuals:
one immature and one adult. The dog is represented by a single immature individual
under eight months of age. Within the size-based categories, both large-sized and small-
sized mammals are represented by one adult and one immature, while the medium-sized
mammal category includes a single adult (Table 2).
Regarding skeletal proﬁles, cranial elements are the most abundant among caprines
(32.87%), consisting mainly of loose teeth. Axial elements are the least frequent (19.4%),
with scapulae being the best represented (9%). The hindlimb is more commonly represented
than the forelimb, with femora and metatarsals being the most frequent elements (9%).
For cattle, the limited number of remains shows a relatively balanced distribution across
different skeletal regions; however, phalanges and tarsal bones are the most represented.
In the case of the dog, both remains correspond to metacarpals (Table A2).
From a taphonomic perspective, the faunal remains exhibit clear evidence of anthro-
pogenic modiﬁcation. A total of 53 bones show traces of butchery, including incisions,
chops, and scraping marks observed on goats, sheep, and cattle, as well as within the
large- and small-sized mammal categories. Skinning marks were identiﬁed on a sheep
metacarpal and metatarsal. Evidence of evisceration was found on caprine and bovine
remains, as well as on large- and small-sized mammals. These consist of deep incisions on
the inner surfaces of ribs, likely related to the removal of viscera from the thoracic cavity.
Similar marks were also documented on the inner surfaces of mandibles, associated with

Carnivore activity on the faunal remains is scarce (NR = 14). The marks attributed to
carnivores include scores, punctures, pits, gnawing, and furrowing, observed on caprine,
bovine, and large-sized mammal bones. On half of the affected remains, cut marks were
also documented on the bone surfaces. This suggests that, following human consumption,
carnivores scavenged the waste left behind (Figure 4).
Among the natural agents affecting the faunal remains, particular attention should
be given to the green and bluish staining observed on 60 bones (Figure 4). This staining
affected the entire surface of most bones, although in a few cases it was only partial.
This colouration occurred post burial, as it overlays cut marks and other taphonomic
modiﬁcations made prior to the bone’s deposit. This patina is the result of copper mineral
dissolution in a humid environment, which allows the mineral to spread throughout the
sediment layer, uniformly staining the bones within it, rather than being conﬁned to a
speciﬁc location within the stratum. In relation to humidity, 41 bones were found to exhibit
manganese oxide staining on their surfaces, and 43 showed calcium carbonate precipitation
(Figure 4). Another signiﬁcant natural agent was plant roots, which left marks on the
surfaces of 65.8% of the bones. Subaerial exposure affected 11.2% of the remains, indicating
a relatively prolonged period of exposure to atmospheric agents prior to burial. Finally,
microorganisms and bacteria acted upon the bone surfaces, causing corrosion on 16.1% of
the specimens.

4.2. Birds
The bird remains consist of a radius, an ulna, and a carpometacarpus from a single
adult duck (see, Table 1). From a taphonomic perspective, all three remains exhibit an-
thropogenic processing marks. These consist of ﬁne, short incisions, both oblique and
transverse, located on the diaphyses of the long bones, indicating deﬂeshing of the animal.
No carnivore marks were observed on the bird bones; however, two of the three remains
show evidence of concretions, root etching, and bluish staining.

4.3. Fishes
A total of 12 ﬁsh remains were documented. Speciﬁcally, three grouper remains
were recovered: one from a white grouper (Epinephelus aeneus) measuring approximately
60 cm in length, one from a dusky grouper (Epinephelus marginatus) of similar size, and
one indeterminate specimen of around 100 cm. Three remains belong to Atlantic blueﬁn
tuna (Thunnus thynnus): one measuring 60 cm and another 110 cm in length. Two remains
were identiﬁed as red porgy or dentex (Pagrus/Sparus), both approximately 70 cm long.
Additionally, one barracuda (Sphyraena sp.) of around 60 cm and a large fragment from
a Sparidae species were recorded. Two further large ﬁsh spines could not be identiﬁed
(Vargas Girón et al., 2024) (see, Table 1).
Taphonomic analysis revealed anthropogenic modiﬁcation on two remains. The ﬁrst
is a caudal vertebra of Atlantic blueﬁn tuna, which displays a transverse cut running
across the posterior portion, from the upper central area to the middle of the posterior
articular surface, indicating that the tuna was segmented. The second case involves a
precaudal vertebra of Atlantic blueﬁn tuna, which is heavily damaged due to a cut that has
removed the entire surface of the left and lower sides of the bone, rendering its taxonomic
attribution uncertain.
Regarding ﬁshing activity, the only evidence of ﬁshing equipment was documented
in SU48. It consists of a bronze ﬁshhook measuring 3 cm in length, 1.4 cm in width, and
between 0.3 and 0.4 cm in thickness. Weighing just 2 g, the piece was found heavily
encrusted, and its state of preservation is poor. The hook also presents a fracture at the tip,
with the barb no longer preserved. The distal end of the shank is pointed, with no evidence
of a line attachment system. The shank has a cylindrical cross-section, while the curvature
appears slightly ﬂattened, suggesting it may have been forged (Vargas Girón et al., 2024)
(Figure 5).

4.4. Molluscs
A total of 148 mollusc remains were documented, corresponding to 116 individual
specimens. The most represented species was the common topshell (Phorcus lineatus), with
41 individuals, followed by the dog cockle (Glycymeris sp.) with 23 specimens, the purple
dye murex (Bolinus brandaris) with 11 individuals, and the grooved carpet shell (Ruditapes
decussatus) with 10 individuals (see, Table 1).
All remains are interpreted as food waste, except for a Glycymeris sp., whose marine
erosion suggests post-mortem collection. Also noteworthy is the presence of a Charonia
lampas, commonly known as a Triton’s trumpet, with its apex removed, suggesting possible
use as an aerophone (Figure 6).

\5. Discussion
Although limited in quantity, the faunal remains analysed represent a valuable source
of information for understanding the foodways and economy of the earliest Phoenician
settlers in Cádiz. By combining archaeozoological and taphonomic approaches with
biochemical analyses, including ancient DNA, a more comprehensive picture of resource
exploitation and maintenance practices can be reconstructed.
Regarding livestock management, there is a clear predominance of caprines (sheep and
goats), which, alongside cattle, formed the primary sources of meat at the Sagasta–Tinte site.
This same pattern is observed at other contemporary sites in Cádiz, such as El Teatro Cómico
(Estaca et al., 2015), Doña Blanca (Morales et al., 1994), and Pocito Chico (Riquelme, 2001).
Among these taxa, a high proportion of immature and juvenile individuals suggests the
consumption of lamb and kid meat. However, the limited number of identiﬁed specimens
means that interpretations must be cautious. The presence of both juvenile and adult
individuals may reﬂect different management practices within the livestock economy,
although the limited size of the assemblage prevents detailed interpretations regarding
speciﬁc exploitation strategies or the relative importance of secondary products. Ducks
were also present and processed for consumption. These birds typically inhabit wetland
environments, both freshwater and saline, and are commonly found in coastal areas. Cádiz
would have provided an ideal habitat for their rearing and consumption. This type of
bird has also been documented in other early Phoenician contexts, such as the site of
Sant’Antioco in Sardinia, where species from coastal environments, such as ﬂamingos and
ducks, have been found, alongside others from marine environments, such as cormorants
(Carenti, 2013).
Notably, no remains of suids or equids were identiﬁed at this site, despite their
presence at other contemporary sites in the region. In the case of suids, although they are
present at Phoenician sites in Gadir, their representation is lower during archaic Phoenician
occupations (Valenzuela-Lamas, 2020). Nevertheless, this absence is likely due to the small
sample size recovered from Sagasta–Tinte. The presence of a dog is conﬁrmed by two
metacarpal bones from a juvenile individual. Dogs are also documented at other Phoenician

https://doi.org/10.3390/humans6030033

<!-- p. 16 -->

Humans 2026, 6, 33 16 of 28

sites, where they may have served as companion animals or for herding livestock (Morales
et al., 1994; Riquelme, 2001; Iborra, 2004; Estaca et al., 2015). Moreover, in Phoenician and
Punic contexts, dogs held signiﬁcant roles not only in the economic sphere but also in ritual
practices (Iborra, 2004; Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas, 2008).
Regarding butchery practices, in addition to ducks, evidence of meat processing has
been documented for both caprine and cattle. Cut marks have been identiﬁed on cranial,
axial, and appendicular elements, indicating that animal processing for consumption took
place within the settlement itself. Among the anthropogenic modiﬁcations, the low number
of burnt bones is particularly noteworthy, as this is a common feature in sites of this period
(Sáez Romero & Belizón Aragón, 2020). This absence is likely related to the methods used to
cook these animals, which may not have involved roasting, an approach that would expose
the bones directly to the ﬂame, but rather boiling, whose effects on bone surfaces are more
difﬁcult to preserve and detect (Roberts et al., 2002; Solari et al., 2015). This type of cooking
can be associated with the utensils documented at other Phoenician sites, such as El Teatro
Cómico, where tannurs, small supports for placing pots over the ﬁre, and various cooking-
related infrastructures have been found (Gener et al., 2014; Sáez Romero & Belizón Aragón,
2020). This interpretation may also be supported by the organic residue analyses conducted
on the ceramic vessels from Sagasta–Tinte, which identiﬁed lipid compounds compatible
with animal products. Although these results do not allow for deﬁnitive identiﬁcation of
speciﬁc foodstuffs, they provide additional evidence for the processing and consumption
of animal-derived resources within the settlement.
Evidence of bone modiﬁcation to produce bone tools has also been documented. The
artefact recovered at Sagasta–Tinte may correspond to a type of pin (possibly a hairpin),
awl, or needle used in textile production. Alike objects have been found at chronologically
comparable sites such as Bajo de la Campana (VVAA, 2025, p. 268) or La Fonteta II
(González Prats, 2014, pp. 239–425). Moreover, based on the morphology and decorative
style of the piece, parallels can be found in contemporary contexts at sites of Carthage
(Kheliﬁ, 2017) and possibly Méndez Núñez (González de Canales et al., 2004, p. 169,
ﬁg. XLIII-11). Also, this type of object continues to appear in later periods (5–4th century
BC), as evidenced in the archaeological record at sites such as Covalta (Blasco Martín, 2015),
El Amarejo (Blasco Martín, 2019, p. 423, ﬁg. 5.376), and El Puig d’Alcoi (Blasco Martín,
2019, p. 477, Figure 5.441), where tools with similar decoration and without ﬁgurative
motifs at the head of the pins have been identiﬁed.
Among the taphonomic traces identiﬁed, there is clear evidence of bone surface
modiﬁcation caused by carnivores. The presence of dogs at the site suggests that they
may have been responsible for the tooth marks observed, likely as scavengers feeding
on refuse discarded by the settlement’s inhabitants. Such modiﬁcations are commonly
found in Phoenician faunal assemblages, as demonstrated at other Iberian Peninsula sites
(e.g., Morales et al., 1994; Riquelme, 2001; Iborra, 2004; Estaca et al., 2015).
Natural agents have also contributed to the alteration of the faunal record. Root
etching, concretion, and the precipitation of manganese oxides are among the most frequent
post-depositional processes affecting the assemblage. Of particular note is the greenish
and bluish staining observed on a signiﬁcant number of bone surfaces, which may be
associated with the use of copper ore for metallurgical purposes at the site (Bashore et al.,
2024; Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas et al., in press-a).
Regarding ﬁshing activity, all the remains analysed at Sagasta–Tinte correspond to
large-sized ﬁsh species (such as tuna, dentex, grouper and barracuda). This pattern is
consistent with other early Phoenician coastal contexts in Andalusia, where similarly large
specimens have been found. These ﬁndings may reﬂect favourable ecological conditions
and access to abundant marine resources, as suggested for other early Phoenician coastal

https://doi.org/10.3390/humans6030033

<!-- p. 17 -->

Humans 2026, 6, 33 17 of 28

contexts in southern Iberia (Sibón, 2004; Córdoba & Ruiz Mata, 2005; Marlasca, 2020;
Vargas Girón et al., 2024). Although the sample from Sagasta–Tinte is not representative in
terms of the quantity or diversity of ﬁsh consumed by the inhabitants of early Phoenician
Cádiz, it nonetheless highlights the signiﬁcance of ﬁshing in Phoenician Gadir. Evidence of
anthropogenic processing has been identiﬁed, including cut marks on two Atlantic blueﬁn
tuna remains, indicating butchery for consumption.
Additionally, the discovery of a bronze ﬁshhook at the site points to the relevance of
ﬁshing activities in the area. The signiﬁcance of this ﬁnding lies primarily in its antiquity,
as very few examples of ﬁshing gear have been recovered from early Phoenician contexts
in Cádiz (Vargas Girón, 2020). Similar ﬁshhooks have been found in early phases at other
sites in the city, such as Teatro Cómico (Vargas Girón et al., 2024) and the excavation at
Calle Ancha 29 (Bernal, 2010). Comparable ﬁnds have also been documented at other
Phoenician settlements in southern Iberia, including La Fonteta (González Prats, 2010),
Morro de Mezquitilla (Mansel, 2000), Toscanos (Maass & Schubart, 1984), and El Cerro del
Villar (Aubet, 1992).
Alongside ﬁshing, mollusc gathering represented one of the major economic activities
documented at the site. The predominance of periwinkles and dog cockles suggests that
shellﬁsh were exploited to meet dietary needs. This type of coastal resource exploitation
is well attested throughout the Mediterranean (e.g., Moreno, 1994; Roselló & Morales,
1994; Carenti & Wilkens, 2006). In addition to food-related remains, a dog cockle specimen
shows signs of marine erosion, indicating post-mortem collection. Furthermore, several
individuals of this species exhibit naturally perforated umbones. However, no use-wear
traces or other modiﬁcations associated with suspension were identiﬁed. Among the
non-culinary ﬁnds, a specimen of Charonia lampas (triton shell) was recovered, which
may have been used as an aerophone. Such instruments are known from antiquity, and
Phoenician-period examples have been documented in industrial contexts in Cádiz, such as
the ﬁsh-salting facility at Puerto-19 (Sáez & Gutiérrez, 2014) and at La Viña de Cantarranas
(Moreno, 1995). They have also been associated with funerary and ritual contexts (Sáez &
Gutiérrez, 2014; Niveau-de-Villedary y Mariñas et al., in press-b).
Although ﬁshing and shellﬁsh gathering activities among archaic Phoenicians are
evident, as demonstrated by the abundant remains and signs of anthropogenic processing,
stable isotope analyses of 13C and 15N conducted on human remains from Mediterranean
Phoenician and Punic sites, such as Can Marines (Salazar-García, 2011), Puig des Molins
(Fuller et al., 2010), Ses Païses (Fuller et al., 2010; Nehlich et al., 2012) in Ibiza, and Kerkouane
(Afat, 2021) in Tunisia, indicate that the diet of these individuals was primarily composed
of terrestrial C3 resources, with a smaller contribution from C4 plants, and only a minor
intake of marine resources (Márquez-Grant & Olivé, 2021).
This apparent discrepancy may reﬂect the different nature of the datasets. While
archaeozoological assemblages document the exploitation of marine resources, stable
isotope analyses provide information on their long-term dietary contribution. Consequently,
the abundance of ﬁsh and shellﬁsh remains does not necessarily imply that marine products
constituted the dominant component of the diet. Instead, marine resources may have played
an important economic role while terrestrial resources continued to provide the principal
dietary contribution.
In this regard, the evidence from Sagasta–Tinte suggests an economy based on the
combined exploitation of livestock, ﬁsh and shellﬁsh resources, reﬂecting the environmental
opportunities offered by the Bay of Cádiz. Similar patterns have been documented at other
Phoenician settlements in the region, such as Doña Blanca (Morales et al., 1994), Pocito
Chico (Riquelme, 2001) and Teatro Cómico (Estaca et al., 2015), where animal husbandry
was complemented by the exploitation of marine resources. Furthermore, archaeological

https://doi.org/10.3390/humans6030033

<!-- p. 18 -->

evidence from other Phoenician sites in Cádiz indicates that ﬁshing and shellﬁsh gathering
formed a recurrent component of local economic practices (Córdoba & Ruiz Mata, 2005;
Marlasca, 2020; Vargas Girón et al., 2024). Therefore, the mixed economy identiﬁed at
Sagasta–Tinte should not be viewed as an exceptional case, but rather as part of a broader
adaptive strategy among Phoenician communities established in coastal environments.
One of the key aspects addressed in this paper is how foodstuffs were processed
and consumed in these societies, as revealed through ceramic organic residue analysis.
The results from Sagasta–Tinte indicate that the preservation conditions for fatty acids
were not optimal due to the acidity of the soil. Aside from fruit-derived acids, only fatty
acids chemically bound to the ceramic matrix were identiﬁed following acid extraction.
Free and absorbed fatty acids were not detected through solvent extraction. Nevertheless,
the identiﬁcation of fatty acids in both vessels suggests the presence of fat or oil. The
combination of palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0), octadecenoic acid isomers (C18:1),
and branched isomers of odd-numbered fatty acids (C15:0, C17:0) may point to the presence
of ruminant fat, either from carcass or dairy, in the Phoenician amphora (SAT39). However,
the secure identiﬁcation of ruminant fat requires compound-speciﬁc isotopic analysis,
which was not undertaken in this case. It is also important to note that microbial activity
cannot be entirely ruled out as a source of these compounds (Dudd et al., 1998). While no
markers of fruit or berries were detected in the large handmade storage vessel (SAT09), the
presence of aldaric acids in the Phoenician amphora (SAT39) suggests the presence of a
fruit product rich in malic acid. Tartaric acid, commonly associated with grape products
when present in signiﬁcant quantities, was not detected.
Regarding the faunal analyses, the organic residue results are consistent with the
processing or storage of animal-derived products in ceramic containers. Animal fats are
among the most frequently detected lipids in organic residues, and their analysis has shown
that ruminant meat was processed in cooking vessels and may also have been transported
in containers such as amphorae (Bison, 2020; Bison et al., 2020; Bonetto et al., 2020; Botto
et al., 2021). In this case, the faunal remains processed at Sagasta–Tinte, mainly goat, sheep,
and cattle, are closely linked to the use and contents of the ceramic assemblage recovered
from the site. This connection provides valuable insights into commensality practices, as
well as the processes of storage and transport of animal products.

\6. Conclusions
This study has highlighted the importance of combining archaeozoological and tapho-
nomic analyses of faunal remains with biochemical research as essential tools for under-
standing the economy of the earliest Phoenician settlers in Gadir.
Although the remains analysed are not numerous, the archaeofaunal results clearly
indicate the presence of a livestock economy primarily based on caprines (goats and sheep),
supplemented by cattle. These animals constituted the main source of animal-derived
food resources at the site, although the limited size of the assemblage prevents detailed
assessment of the role of secondary products. The signiﬁcance of these species is evident
not only in their processing and consumption at the site but also in their potential storage
and transport in ceramic containers. This is supported by the organic residue analysis of
a Phoenician amphora from the site, which revealed the presence of ruminant fatty acids
alongside fruit-derived acids.
These ﬁndings shed light on food practices and the maintenance strategies employed
by the Phoenician groups in the early city of Gadir. It is plausible that meat products were
preserved in storage vessels for later consumption or transport. Among the meat resources,
the absence of equids and suids is notable, as these species are typically present in the
subsistence strategies of such groups. In the case of Sagasta–Tinte, this absence is likely due

https://doi.org/10.3390/humans6030033

<!-- p. 19 -->

Humans 2026, 6, 33 19 of 28

to the limited number of remains recovered. Nevertheless, the presence of these species in
early Phoenician contexts is generally minor.
Ancient DNA analysis did not allow for the determination of the origins or signatures
of local adaptation/selection of the two cattle specimens; it did enable the sexing of one
sample, an aspect that could not have been conﬁrmed through traditional osteometric
methods due to the scarcity of measurable diagnostic bones.
Marine resources also played a signiﬁcant role in the Phoenician economy of Gadir, as
evidenced by the presence of large ﬁsh species such as tuna, dentex, grouper, barracuda, and
wreckﬁsh, all showing signs of anthropogenic processing. This highlights the importance
of ﬁshing as an economic activity within the settlement, further supported by the discovery
of a ﬁshhook speciﬁcally intended for this purpose.
Additional evidence of coastal exploitation comes from the mollusc remains, partic-
ularly periwinkles and dog cockles, which indicate shellﬁsh gathering along the Cádiz
coastline during this period. Some specimens appear to have been collected for purposes
beyond consumption. Notably, a triton shell may have served as a sonorous instrument,
a practice attested in other Phoenician contexts. The presence of duck remains bearing
anthropogenic marks also points to the exploitation of coastal and wetland environments
for acquiring such resources. In this regard, Cádiz would have provided an ideal habitat
for the rearing and consumption of this species. Altogether, these ﬁndings underscore the
importance of aquatic resources in archaic Phoenician settlements in the Bay of Cádiz.
Taken together, the archaeozoological, taphonomic and biomolecular evidence sup-
ports the existence of a mixed economy in which livestock husbandry, ﬁshing and shellﬁsh
gathering all contributed to the subsistence strategies of the earliest Phoenician communi-
ties established in Gadir. Organic residue analyses further suggest that animal products may
have been stored or transported in ceramic containers, highlighting the value of integrating
multiple lines of evidence to investigate Phoenician foodways.
