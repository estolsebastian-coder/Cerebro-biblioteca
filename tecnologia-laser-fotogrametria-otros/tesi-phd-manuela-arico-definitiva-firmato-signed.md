Introduction

Research background and justification

Architectural Heritage (AH) management frequently encounters significant difficulties

in documenting existing historical buildings, due to the lack or the loss of information,

inefficient geometric representation, and unrecorded restorations over time. Moreover,

the traditional methods for supporting evidence based on archival research and

bidimensional deliverables have been proven to be time-consuming and incomplete;

these inconveniences often lead to poorly planned interventions, loss of resources and

increased costs for maintenance.

As the expected life cycle of the historical buildings to be maintained is considered

potentially perpetual (Quattrini et al., 2017), their correct management has become

nowadays even more important, with an increased need for reliable, strategic and

structured tools capable of handling the complexity of historical assets (Santagati et

al., 2018). From this point of view, three-dimensional digitisations, founded on a

thorough knowledge of the buildings to be recorded, are ideal to fulfil the scopes of

planned maintenance and potential restoration procedures.

In the last 15 years, especially in Europe, Cultural Heritage (CH) researchers became

interested in the novelties introduced by Building Information Modelling (BIM), trying

to extend its capabilities to existing buildings of historical value, with the name of

Heritage Building Information Modelling (HBIM). As a derivation of BIM, HBIM as

well is an object-oriented modelling solution, where the parametric models are

composed by geometric elements and semantic attributes and constitute at the same

time a computational and informative database (Volk et al., 2014).

The main advantage of using HBIM processes to manage a CH asset is that the model

not only reproduces the aspect and the behaviour of the real structures, but it even

contains a conceptual system of information inside the BIM environment. Another

aspect appealing to CH management is the collaborative work among different

professionals, enhancing multidisciplinary knowledge and skills during the whole

process.

HBIM documentary connotation and the synergic concept combined together bring

huge advantages to the management, conservation, and restoration of buildings of

historical values (Pocobelli et al., 2018; Lopez et al., 2018; Lovell et al., 2023).

Whenever a HBIM process is based on the outputs of preliminary surveys carried out

on the case studies with Massive Data Capture Systems (MDCSs), such as Terrestrial

Laser Scanning (TLS) and/or terrestrial and aerial photogrammetry, this approach is

called Scan-to-BIM (Brumana et al., 2020). This approach is strongly recommended

in HBIM process, because is the most reliable, quick, efficient, non-invasive and cost-

effective method for capturing the real surfaces of the assets, guarantee the maximum

accuracy of the data and provide all necessary information for parametric modelling

(Aricò and Lo Brutto, 2022; Costantino et al., 2021). The obtained modelled replica can

be exploited in all parts to build a 3D smart model and understand its state of

conservation.

These are the reasons why Scan-to-BIM is considered the most suitable approach for

HBIM, even if the methodology is relatively new and still requires a clear framework

especially for quantitative and qualitative evaluations of the final model.

Objectives of the research

This research originated from three theoretical questions about HBIM methodology

through a Scan-to-BIM approach:

\1. How can a digital model match the AH requirements?

\2. Is data capture based on MDCSs genuinely exhaustive and representative of the

complex reality of an existing building?

\3. How can manual modelling procedures be improved without resorting to complicated

algorithms or experimental and resource-consuming techniques?

In order to provide their related answers, the illustrated research was carried out with

the main objective of making the HBIM digitisation through a Scan-to-BIM approach a

vehicle of documentation, information and communication which could support and

enable a correct comprehension and the awareness on the AH assets, fostering

improved conservation and restoration plans and empowering the collaboration among

operators involved in their management.

\1. Methodology

1.1 Introduction to Building Information Modelling

Building Information Modelling (BIM) (Van Nederveen and Tolman, 1992) is an

integrated process which digitises a 3D model to plan, design, construct, and manage

a building throughout its lifecycle. This model serves as both a visual representation

and a data repository, containing all physical and functional details. Beyond geometry,

it includes semantic metadata to enhance collaboration among professionals. Whilst

definitions might vary, them all acknowledge that BIM fundamentally centralises

information in a single database to improve cooperation in the construction process

(Figure 1).

Figure 1. Life cycle of a building through BIM management.

1.1.1 Evolution of BIM

The rise of Computer Aided Design (CAD) in the 1970s led to debates on representing

reality in design, inspiring a model-based approach which included both geometry and

related data. In 1974, Charles Eastman from Carnegie-Mellon University introduced

the concept of a "virtual model" for buildings through his Building Description System

1

<!-- p. 18 -->

Methodology

(BDS) (Eastman et al., 1974); this computer-based database was aimed at addressing

the limitations of 2D drawings by linking geometric and spatial data. BDS could

automatically generate and update drawings, ensuring consistency and efficiency in

the management of building documentation (Eastman et al., 1974). In 1975, he further

developed his idea, describing how computerized modelling could use parametric

objects (such as walls and windows) from libraries to build a 3D model. Each object

contained detailed information on materials, suppliers, and specifications (Eastman et

al., 1975).

Eastman’s work laid the foundation for modern BIM. In 1984, Gábor Bojár developed

for the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) sector the Radar CH software

(later ArchiCAD) by his Graphisoft1 company in Budapest, introducing "smart objects"

which could be customised and updated in both 3D and 2D. In 1997, Leonid Raiz and

Irwin Jungreis created Pro/Engineer, which evolved into Revit (from “REVise Instantly”)

by Autodesk in 2000. Revit revolutionized BIM by enabling parametric modelling

without programming and introducing time-based simulations, solidifying its role as a

leading tool in architecture, engineering, and construction.

1.1.2 BIM definitions

As BIM adoption grew, defining its terminology became essential. Since 2002, the

meaning of the "M" in BIM has evolved from "Modelling" to also include "Model"

(representing the 3D structure) and "Management" (focusing on information and

process control). Whilst "Modelling" remains the most common term, there is no

universally accepted definition.

Various organizations have provided key definitions:

✓ U.S. General Services Administration (GSA), 2007: A multi-faceted digital model

used to document, simulate construction, and manage operations (General

Services Administration, 2007).

1.1.3 The BIM environment

BIM is more than just software or a 3D model; it serves as a shared database for

managing a building’s entire lifecycle, from construction to demolition. This database

includes information about building components and their behaviour.

BIM models can be single (focused on one discipline) or federated (integrating multiple

sub-models from different fields like architecture, structure, and systems). These

models contain intelligent objects, called Building Object Models (BOMs), with

parametric rules which allow automatic geometric adjustments, while maintaining

relationships across three levels:

\1. Within-object relations: Interactions between elements within the same object (e.g.,

reinforcement inside a column).

\2. Peer-object relations: Adjustments in one object based on changes in another (e.g.,

a wall adjusting to a door).

3

<!-- p. 20 -->

Methodology

\3. Hierarchical relations: Global modifications that impact the entire model based on

reference lines, axes, and planes.

BOMs are organized into families, characterised by model categories (structural and

non-structural elements like walls, doors, and floors) and annotation categories (text,

dimensions, notes). These categories are further divided into classes and subclasses,

which define common attributes and allow user modifications through parameters.

Families are characteristic tools of any BIM software, albeit they are more extensively

used in Autodesk Revit (Garagnani and Cinti Luciani, 2011). Regardless the authoring

software, there are three types of families: System families, Component (Loadable)

families, and In-place families.

✓ System families are built into the software, cannot be created or removed, and follow

a strict hierarchy.

✓ Component (Loadable) families are user-created from templates available in the

software, stored separately as .rfa files, and can be reused in different projects.

These offer full customization at all levels (even for the superclass and class), for

parametric and freeform shapes.

✓ In-place families are custom elements created for a specific project and cannot be

reused elsewhere.

Modifications within BIM models occur through Type parameters (affecting all

instances of a type) and Instance parameters (applying to individual objects). This

structured approach ensures flexibility and consistency in managing building

components (Figures 2 and 3).

1.1.4 Levels of informational definition

BIM methodology enables construction professionals to create models suited to their

expertise and project requirements. A key aspect is defining the Level of Development

(LoD), which specifies the required dimensional and informational detail, differing from

traditional drawings where detail depends on scale and project phase. Various

international standards define levels of BIM model development, collectively known as

LOx, which include:

✓ Model Progression Specification (MPS)

✓ Level of Development (LOD)

✓ Level of Detail (LOd)

✓ Level of Geometry (LOG)

✓ Level of Information (LOI)

✓ Level of Information Need (LOIN)

These classifications vary by country and standard, leading to different interpretations.

Below are some key definitions from various sources:

AEC(UK) BIM Protocol (2012Akpinar): Based on Level of Detail (LOd), emphasizing

geometric aspects only, divided in four categories from schematic to realistic grades

(G0-G3) (AEC(UK), 2012);

PAS 1192-2:2013 (UK)2: Introduced Level of Definition (LoD), covering both graphical

(LOG) and informational (LOI) components with a seven-grade scale (G1-G7).

1.1.7 Interoperability

Interoperability is a key feature of BIM, enabling interdisciplinary collaboration by

integrating data from various disciplines into a shared platform. This allows

professionals to work together efficiently, minimizing errors and ensuring consistency

(Sacchi, 2016).

Work-sharing allows multiple users to collaborate on the same model simultaneously

by dividing it into worksets based on parts, roles, or disciplines (Figure 7). This ensures

that each task is assigned to the most qualified team member. The central (or

federated) model is stored on a server or cloud, enabling real-time synchronization.

Team members work on local copies and update the central model, ensuring

consistency across the project and minimising errors or inconsistencies caused by

miscommunication (Dalla Mora et al., 2014).

Work-sharing is categorized into:

✓ Closed BIM (Lonely BIM): Uses the same software, with the model saved in its

native format.

✓ Open BIM: Uses different software, exporting models in open, neutral formats to

ensure compatibility.

For Open BIM, models must be exported in formats which preserve component data

for seamless sharing. Since the 1990s, buildingSMART International (bSI)

(buildingSMART International) has regulated Open BIM through the IFC (Industry

Foundation Classes) standard. Recognized as an ISO 16739:2013 standard, IFC

ensures accurate export of model attributes in an interoperable format, based on the

EXPRESS4 language and organized into four interconnected layers. IFC models a

hierarchical structure, adding layers of detail to objects and their relationships.

1.2. The HBIM extension

Architectural Heritage (AH) management faces challenges in documenting historical

buildings due to missing information, inefficient geometric representation, and

unrecorded restorations. Traditional archival research and 2D documentation are often

incomplete and time-consuming, leading to poorly planned interventions, resource

loss, and higher maintenance costs.

As historical buildings require long-term maintenance, strategic and structured tools

are essential (Santagati et al., 2021). Three-dimensional digitization, based on

thorough knowledge of the buildings, offers an effective solution for planned

maintenance and restoration.

Over the past 15 years, particularly in Europe, researchers have explored the potential

of Heritage Building Information Modelling (HBIM), an extension of BIM tailored for

historical structures. As a derivation of BIM, HBIM as well provides parametric models

with geometric and semantic attributes (Volk et al., 2014; Allegra et al., 2020), serving

as a comprehensive database for documentation, analysis, and project development

(Rocha et al., 2020).

The key benefit of HBIM is its ability to replicate both the physical structure and the

embedded information within a collaborative digital environment. It fosters

multidisciplinary cooperation, ensuring that decisions are interconnected and aligned

with long-term master plans, preventing short-sighted choices. This integrated

approach significantly enhances the management, conservation, and restoration of

historical buildings (Pocobelli et al., 2018).

1.2.1 Definitions of HBIM and state of the art

HBIM was first introduced in 2007 at the 8th International Symposium on Virtual Reality,

Archaeology, and Intelligent Cultural Heritage by Maurice Murphy, Eugene McGovern,

and Sara Pavia from the Dublin Institute of Technology and Trinity College Dublin

(Murphy et al., 2007). In 2009, they defined HBIM as “a new system for modelling

historic structures by mapping parametric objects onto point cloud and image survey

data” (Murphy et al., 2009). In 2012, M. Murphy proposed in his doctoral thesis an

HBIM workflow to document Irish neoclassical architecture (1700–1830) through five

case studies in Dublin’s Henrietta Street (Murphy, 2012).

This method combined Laser Scanning (LS) for data collection and extensive archival

research to create a parametric library applicable to similar structures. During the

following year, he further described this parametric modelling approach as a digital

library of historical architectural elements, reinforcing HBIM’s reliance on parametric

object libraries (Dore and Murphy, 2012) and digital survey techniques (Dore and

Murphy, 2013).

Murphy’s HBIM definition has been widely acknowledged in research not just as a

modelling tool but a proper framework (Fai et al., 2011), covering all workflow phases

from data acquisition to the semantic enrichment of digital model (Saygi and

Remondino, 2013; Volk et al., 2014; Maiezza and Tata, 2021). The application of BIM

to AH has been validated through numerous studies showcasing successful

digitisations of Heritage buildings (Rocha et al., 2020). Some researchers have

focused on specific HBIM aspects, such as data collection, processing, modelling

techniques, the role of HBIM in long-term AH management (Historic England, 2017),

and the quality of these digitisations (Pritchard et al., 2021). However, other scholars

highlighted current gaps and limitations in the process (Penjor et al., 2024;).

1.2.2 Similarities and Differences between BIM and HBIM

Being a derivation of BIM, HBIM shares some fundamental aspects, although applied

to different contexts: indeed, HBIM as well relies on intelligent parametric models to

represent buildings or infrastructures, enabling the management and analysis of

architectural and structural information (Antonopoulou and Bryan, 2017).

These digital copies must be created in such a way which accurately represent the

modelled items, facilitate information enrichment (i.e. adding material properties and

construction techniques), and foster assessment and data interpretation. Furthermore,

they should promote multidisciplinary among the various professionals, allow data

sharing and interoperability and provide the possibility to perform simulations to

support informed decision-making.

However, whilst BIM is about new constructions to manage their limited life cycle from

design to an eventual decommission, HBIM is focused on the documentation,

preservation, and restoration of existing assets of historical and architectural value,

thus characterised by a potentially perpetual life cycle (Quattrini et al., 2017). From an

ontological perspective, parametric objects in HBIM should address the "what," "why,"

and "how" of each modelled element (Biagini et al., 2016). Differently from the as-

designed BIM items, the as-built conditions of an artifact feature irregular shapes,

handcrafted decorations, asymmetries and imperfections which cannot be easily

represented.

To capture the as-is geometry of existing buildings and to enhance their digital

reconstruction HBIM methodology increasingly relies on digital surveys, which is often

the only way to get updated information on an historical asset. The data acquisition is

often supported by archival research for increasing knowledge, understanding and

awareness about the structures to be digitised.

BIM is based onto extensive libraries of standardised items, generally in-built and

Whilst BIM has been strictly regulated worldwide since the beginning (see paragraphs

HBIM requires a broader work team than BIM, including historians, archaeologists and

conservationists alongside professionals closely related to AEC sector.

1.2.3 Modelling of the irregular items and decays

The significant semantic and geometric heterogeneity of construction elements within

existing AH makes them difficult to be accurately modelled in a BIM environment.

These elements often have irregularities due to changes over time and past

construction methods.

Many studies have focused on solutions for parametric and informational modelling of

AH, with a special focus on translating survey data into HBIM and creating specific

libraries. Some researchers, like Murphy et al., have created libraries based on

architectural rules from historical texts, particularly from the Classic or Neoclassic ages

(Daniels and Georgopoulos, 2023; Sakellaris et al., 2022) and the Renaissance

(Murphy et al., 2015; Apollonio et al., 2012). However, this approach becomes almost

as real elements often differ significantly.

Old construction methods, such as masonry or steam-bent timber, don’t follow modern

standards, creating geometric inaccuracies which require to be represented in the

digital model. These inaccuracies can involve irregular geometry, such as out-of-plumb

or tapered walls, deformed arches, variable dimensions of elements, and misaligned

surfaces. These aspects differ from traditional BIM tools and need special solutions for

accurate representation.

Fortunately, HBIM tools can address these issues. Nowadays, BIM environments

provide strategies for the manual modelling of non-conventional geometries without

recurring to in-built families and commands. Sometimes, custom families need to be

created from scratch, which is a time-consuming process requiring advanced skills.

Adaptive parameters which allow geometrical alterations are a rather preferred

approach; they can be applied to asymmetrical, irregular or deformed surfaces and

volumes. They are particularly useful for mapping material decays (cracks, structural

failures or damages) within the digital model, for restauration purposes.

Deformations or damages can be shown as parametric families containing information

about the type of decay. BIM tools can also integrate the condition of a structure into a

standard database.

Another approach is layered modelling, where changes over time or restoration plans

are displayed on separate layers without affecting the general model, allowing a

multitemporal view of the asset for better awareness and maintenance.

Quattrini et al., 2015; Fai and Rafeiro, 2014). While this method is effective, it is time-

consuming and requires external software, so interoperability is necessary.

To overcome this consumption of resources, many researchers are currently

1.2.4 HBIM international reference standards

The HBIM process should be traceable, reliable, and unambiguous, ensuring that

metadata enhances asset interpretation and management. International reference

systems serve as standardized frameworks for implementing HBIM worldwide and

establishing common methodologies for its whole workflow, ensuring consistency and

interoperability.

The first efforts to establish those standards were inspired by UNESCO’s Charter on

the Conservation of Digital Heritage (2003) and began formal codification in 2009 with

the London Charter, later integrated into the Principles of Seville (2012). Although

initially focused on archaeology and for restauration purposes only, these guidelines

set ethical standards for digital visualization in AH, emphasizing intellectual and

scientific transparency. Nowadays they widely are applied also for documentation

purposes.

These principles were reinforced by the Vancouver Declaration (2015) and officially

ratified at the ICOMOS General Assembly (2017). That same year, Historic England

published BIM for Heritage, a guideline derived in part from ISO 19650, addressing

interoperability, information exchange, and collaboration for historic buildings. This built

upon prior work by Historic Scotland (2014) and aimed to bridge gaps in BIM’s

application to historical assets.

A formal structure for describing non-graphical attributes and mutual relationships

between modelled items to be used for AH documentation was provided in 2006 by the

Intercultural Centre of Documentation (CIDOC) within a Conceptual Reference Model

(CRM) in ISO 21127, with a common semantic framework on which historical

information can be mapped; whilst the Comité International de Photogrammétrie

Architecturale (CIPA) and the International Centre for restauration of Built Heritage

(ICCROM) set best practises for ensuring accuracy in digital surveys.

The European Union sought to integrate these various protocols into an interoperable

platform based on open standard semantic web formats, supporting several initiatives

1.2.6 Semantics and ontologies

In addition to geometric content, HBIM models require a set of information appropriate

to the purposes for which they were created.

Specifically, this information must be structured to establish logical relationships

between architectural elements, their organization and function within the model.

Ontologies are hierarchical structures which connect heterogeneous data

(bibliographic or archival sources, images, surveys, or other types of documents) to

HBIM models through a unique and easily interpretable representation. In this way,

each element is identified by attributes assigned during the semantic enrichment phase

(such as typology, materials, construction techniques, possible mappings, etc.),

making it easier to search for and extract related information. There are vocabularies,

such as the Getty Art & Architecture Thesaurus (AAT), which allow for the cataloguing

of modelled elements and the assignment of a Cultural Heritage Object Code ID to

each of them (Lopez et al., 2018).

At a larger scale, ontologies in HBIM enhance information accessibility and ensure

more precise and interoperable documentation. To prevent misinterpretations, various

standards and guidelines exist (though not a true regulatory framework) depending on

the type of semantic attributes included in the model:

archaeology;

2.2 Massive Data Capture Systems

Over the past 20 years, advancements in Geomatics have introduced a wide range of

data acquisition technologies tailored to meet the specific needs of different heritage

artifacts. The selection of a surveying method depends on several factors, including

the scope of investigations, dimensions of the site, complexity, desired accuracy, cost,

time constraints, the optimisation of the process, etc (Pepe et al., 2022).

Given these variables, large-scale 3D digitisation requires versatile, reliable, and

efficient solutions (Zlot et al., 2014). One of the AH requirements specifically points out

how these methodologies must always be not invasive and not destructive

(Moropoulou et al., 2013). Rather than competing, different survey techniques should

be integrated to overcome individual limitations in accuracy and detail (Rinaudo and

Scolamiero, 2021), ultimately leading to more coherent and refined input (Mateus et

al., 2019; Buill et al., 2020; Ebolese et al., 2019) for an improved HBIM parametric

modelling (Alshawabkeh and Baik, 2023).

The following sections will explore the primary surveying technologies, starting from

the terrestrial laser scanning, and then going on with photogrammetry carried out with

both terrestrial and aerial-based approach.

2.3 The Terrestrial Laser Scanning

In the terrestrial laser scanning the acquisition process, carried out automatically and

regardless of the morphology of the object, produces a digital output called “point

cloud”, which consists of a dataset of metric coordinates for points in space. These

coordinates are calculated by measuring azimuth and zenith angles and the inclined

distance between the object and the sensor, referred to the geometric centre of the

instrument. Conceptually, these devices are like total stations used in topographic

surveying, but with the ability to continuously acquire millions of points per second

rather than individual points measured separately.

When laser scanners are equipped with a colour camera, RGB values are added to

the geometric coordinates x,y,z, increasing acquisition times due to the need for image

capture. Currently, various types of laser scanners are available on the market, each

with specific characteristics regarding acquisition methods, precision5, accuracy6,

resolution7, range and operational speed, which depend on the desired quality and

resolution. Despite their differences, laser sensors can be classified into two main

categories based on the measurement variables: Time of Flight (ToF) Laser Scanners,

whenever they operate by emitting a short laser pulse, or Phase Shift Laser Scanners,

if measurements are calculated from the shift between the emitted and received

signals of a continuous modulated wave.

Compared to ToF devices, the latter ones provide higher accuracy (2-3 mm), faster

acquisition speed and denser point clouds, but cover a limited range (~100 meters

effective, up to 300 meters max)8.

A further classification of distance-measuring laser scanners pertains to the data

acquisition method, which can be divided into two types: TLS can be performed either

with instruments in fixed positions (static) or with instruments in motion (mobile).

2.3.2 Mobile Laser Scanning

Mobile Mapping Systems (MMSs) enable real-time, continuous data acquisition by

tracking the scanner’s trajectory within a scene (Sammartano and Spanò, 2018; Gollob

et al., 2020). Typically mounted on vehicles or worn as backpacks, these systems rely

on Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receivers and Inertial Measurement

Units (IMUs) to determine position and distance, allowing for the digital reconstruction

of environments. However, in confined spaces where GNSS signals are unavailable

because of a weakness in the signal (Del Perugia et al., 2019), Handheld Mobile Laser

Scanners (HMLSs) are preferred, which don’t necessarily need a GNSS for

acquisitions (Bauwens et al., 2016).

HMLSs utilize Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) algorithms, which

enable real-time mapping and self-localization by measuring laser beam return times

to generate a 3D point cloud. Additional motion sensors track movement speed and

orientation, continuously updating the scanned environment (Alsadik and Karam,

<!-- p. 45 -->

The Scan-to-BIM approach

2021). Compared to TLS, HMLSs offer significant advantages in speed and efficiency,

as the point’s measurement itself takes less time to be calculated, and they allow for

continuous scanning rather than requiring multiple static captures (Wu et al., 2022).

This reduces data acquisition time and minimizes occlusion effects (Nikoohemat et al.,

2017; Zlot et al., 2014).

Regardless their performance and accuracy are related to their technical

specifications, whatever the device used, it always delivers an output in the form a

point cloud containing metrical information where data are reported in their x,y,z

coordinates and sometimes in their RGB values as acquired whenever a camera is

integrated to these systems.

Furthermore, HMLS’ more compact size and extreme portability is advantageous for

the uninterrupted and more uniform coverage of scenes in few paths, above all for the

detection of confined, narrow or occluded places (Akpinar, 2021; Xin et al., 2022;

Ryding et al., 2015).

Walking paths for HMLS surveys should be always accurately planned before any

operational phases, as pre-determined routes enable faster and more efficient

acquisitions on-site. A key requirement is ensuring significant overlap between mutual

paths to improve alignment accuracy. Paths should also be set at a certain distance

from vertical surfaces to maintain a consistent laser beam inclination. Reference points

must be accurately placed, as they help improve path alignment and ensure full area

coverage. It is recommended that reference points be shared by multiple paths and

that paths form a closed loop for improved accuracy of the final point cloud.

The SLAM algorithms, beyond enabling real-time calculations of the paths and

acquisitions, enable the shape recognition of the captured elements on simple

primitives such as horizontal and vertical planes and cylindrical items (Buratowski et

al., 2022). This feature is helpful whenever the environment is characterised by either

varied configurations such as dense vegetation (Cabo et al., 2018; Shao et al., 2020),

uneven and multilevel landscapes, or scarce geometric references such as organic

underground spaces (D'Agostino et al., 2022; Di Stefano et al., 2021).

These geometric primitives are used also for evaluating the propagation of positioning

errors within the same SLAM algorithms (Del Duca and Machado, 2023). Despite these

2.4 The photogrammetric survey

Photogrammetry use passive optical measurement to analyse and reconstruct objects

through a dataset of photographs taken from various positions. This cost-effective and

efficient method relies on the principle that images are scaled-down central projections

of objects. For accurate 3D reconstruction, each point of the object must be visible in

at least two images.

A recommended preliminary step for any photogrammetric survey is an accurate

planning phase, which must consider both the shape and size of the asset to be

surveyed and its position in relation to the surrounding environment. A critical aspect

of the planning is calculating the Ground Sampling Distance (GSD), which represents

the actual area of the surface covered by a single pixel of the image; it depends on

the camera-to-object distance, the focal length, and the pixel size of the camera

sensor. Specifically, the GSD increases with a longer camera-to-object distance and

decreases with a longer focal length.

The photogrammetric processing workflow typically includes Image Orientation, Dense

Point Cloud production, Mesh Calculation, Texture Calculation, and Orthophoto

Production (Dostal and Yamafune, 2018). This workflow is generally characterized by

"Structure from Motion" (SfM) algorithms, typical of the field of Computer Vision, that

enable an initial image orientation through the identification of key points in individual

photos, calculating the approximate parameters of the images, and correlating

recognizable points across multiple images, until their spatial coordinates are

determined. At the end, a sparse 3D point cloud, enriched with colorimetric

information, is obtained. SfM simplifies the process by eliminating the need for

exhaustive camera calibration data (Martinez-Carricondo et al., 2021).

The SfM process is integrated with a “bundle adjustment” process to recalculate the

image orientation parameters. In this step several Ground Control Points (GCPs)

whose coordinates are known from a topographic or GNSS survey are used. This

further process provides the correct exterior orientation parameters of the camera.

After tis step, a new point cloud, denser and metrically referenced, is finally achieved,

to be used as it is or further processed through a polygonal procedure based on

meshing algorithms, such as the Delaunay algorithm. The eventually resulting

triangular surface allows for an additional stage of photorealistic representation: using

texture mapping techniques, photographic textures extracted from the original images

are projected onto the mesh model, creating a fully realistic 3D model. From this

model, it is also possible to derive 2D orthophotos useful for describing specific areas.

This image-based survey methodology is particularly advantageous for the 3D

reconstruction of Architectural Heritage assets due to its acquisition speed, low

equipment costs, and wide range of applications.

Depending on the methods and the equipment used for capturing the initial set of

images, photogrammetry could be roughly divided into terrestrial and aerial one.

2.4.1 The terrestrial photogrammetric survey

Terrestrial photogrammetry involves capturing images with ground-supported

cameras, typically Digital Single Lens Reflex (DSLR) cameras, either handheld, tripod-

mounted, or pole-mounted. This method includes two primary capture techniques:

stereoscopic and convergent.

Stereoscopic captures maintain a parallel camera-to-object distance with the optical

axis perpendicular to the surface. The planning of these captures is based on factors

such as focal length and image overlap. Key calculated parameters include image

scale, GSD, and forward and lateral overlap. When stereoscopic captures are

impractical, convergent captures are used, where the camera follows a circular or

semicircular path around the object with a consistent orientation toward a central point.

2.4.2 The aerial photogrammetric survey

For areas which are difficult or impossible to access using TLS or terrestrial

photogrammetry, close-range aerial photogrammetry with Unmanned Aerial Vehicles

(UAVs) is often integrated into the survey. This method has become widely used in BH

documentation, offering versatility, high metric reliability, and reduced execution times

(Lo Brutto et al., 2017; Pepe et al., 2022).

UAVs provide operational flexibility, covering large areas quickly and capturing high-

resolution data from multiple perspectives. They are commonly used to complement

missing information from other surveys. When combined with terrestrial

photogrammetry, UAVs utilize Multi View Stereo (MVS) algorithms to densify the point

cloud generated by the Structure from Motion (SfM) approach (Pepe and Costantino,

2021). UAV surveys require minimal planning time and are typically conducted

automatically via remote control applications, though manual operation is also

possible.

Like terrestrial photogrammetry, UAV photogrammetry features two main capture

techniques: convergent and stereoscopic. Convergent captures involve tilting the

camera relative to the object, following a circular or elliptical trajectory. They can be

further classified as parallel convergent (aligned with façade planes) or inclined

convergent (angled captures). Stereoscopic captures maintain a constant camera-to-

object distance with the optical axis perpendicular to the surface, particularly useful for

surveying upper façades and flat roofs. In such cases, a nadiral optical axis (vertically

downward) is used.

Key stereoscopic parameters include image scale, GSD, and forward and lateral

overlap. Different drones were used for the three case studies in this research

(Chapters 2, 4, and 5), and their specifications will be detailed in the respective

chapters.

2.5 Challenges and Limitations in digital surveying: A
Critical Overview

TLS, HMLS and both terrestrial and aerial photogrammetry have proved as essential

methods for acquiring the as-built accurate geometric information and as-is conditions

for AH whenever other types of documentation are lacking or missing. Despite their

significant advantages, these technologies present specific challenges, limitations and

drawbacks, which must be critically understood to ensure their effective application.

This section provides an in-depth discussion of the technical, operational, and

methodological issues inherent in these digital survey approaches. At the end of this

analysis, an integrated survey diversely combining these techniques trying to balance

the available resources and budget, the scope of work and the project’s scale, the site’s

conditions and the desired accuracy of the final datasets will be discussed as the most

appropriate strategy for the Scan-to-BIM digitisations presented in this defence.

2.5.2 TLS with static devices: accuracy versus practical
constraints

Static TLS delivered dense point clouds with millimetric accuracy, making it particularly

suitable for HBIM digitisations. Nevertheless, the method entailed significant

operational constraints.

Due to operational constraints, the method - being static and capable of capturing only

one scan at a time from a fixed position- required repeated application. The laser

scanner had to be repositioned multiple times to cover the entire area and to achieve

the target resolution of the final point cloud (Wu et al., 2022). It required a careful

planning of scan positions to minimize occlusions and ensure full spatial coverage,

with different settings for each acquisition, which had to be forecast before the proper

operational phase; plus the minimal operational time required for data acquisition,

combined with downtime needed to relocate the device between successive positions.

This method is usually discouraged for extensive environments, because of a longer

time of acquisition and large, burdensome datasets which were computationally

demanding to process. Moreover, the accuracy of static LS resulted highly sensitive to

environmental factors, especially to dense vegetation which affected complete

coverage of the assets and the readability of the point clouds. This has been

experienced in San Giovanni dei Lebbrosi and some buildings in the complex of San

Giovanni degli Eremiti, where some walls have shown altered colours due to the

shadowing effects from near tall trees, and removal of noise brought by climbing plants

caused gaps in the cluster.

2.5.3 HMLS: improved mobility at the cost of accuracy

HMLS systems, based on SLAM algorithms, drastically reduce fieldwork time and

simplify access to environments characterised by a wide extension, uneven terrains,

dense vegetation or eventual occlusions (Aricò et al., 2023a). They are even more

compact and portable (Sammartano and Spanò, 2018), with no downtime for carrying

the device.

However, the advantages of mobility come with notable limitations which should be

addressed prior to acquisitions. The SLAM-based positioning system is inherently

prone to drift, particularly during long, unlooped trajectories, whilst the accuracy of the

resulting point cloud is therefore lower than the static TLS, typically ranging around 1

cm. Furthermore, the point density by HMLS relies on walking speed of the operators

which should be kept constant and free from obstacles, and results anyway lower and

less homogeneous, affecting the ability to capture fine details. These inconveniences

may not be acceptable for all applications.

Given the peculiar environmental conditions in the complexes of San Giovanni degli

Eremiti and Santa Maria della Grotta, and the scope of work of the related digitisations

(respectively a study on the morphology of terrain and a HBIM model), an accuracy of

1 cm was tolerated. To improve the application of HMLS, stable and close trajectories

have been designed, preventing loops and planning generous overlaps between paths

which would have helped the algorithm to shape the scans in real time.

combine geometric reconstruction with high-resolution texture mapping and is a cost-

effective solution.

could not be surveyed with other methodologies: the roofing systems of San Giovanni

dei Lebbrosi, Santissima Trinità di Delia, and Santa Maria della Grotta. In the latter

case, it has been fundamental even for obtaining the textured orthophotos of the long

walls of the building (from external and internal façades).

2.5.5 Challenges and implications of method integration

One of the main challenges in digital surveying lied in the integration of data acquired

from different sources. Combining TLS and photogrammetric data yielded highly

comprehensive models, but it also introduced problems of alignment, consistency, and

scale, as explained in the following section dedicated to data processing in this chapter.

Another important issue regarded the translation of raw survey data into semantically

meaningful models. Whilst laser scanning and photogrammetry produced

geometrically accurate meshes or point clouds, they didn’t inherently include

information about material properties, construction techniques, or historical phases.

This limited their direct integration into HBIM environments, which relied on parametric

objects enriched with semantic attributes. Developing reliable methods to convert

of research.

Digital survey technologies undoubtedly were fundamental in the documentation and

analysis of architectural and archaeological features of the case studies. However,

their application had to be accompanied by a critical understanding of their limitations.

Static TLS offered unmatched accuracy but was time-intensive and logistically

demanding. HMLS provided mobility and speed but sacrificed geometric precision and

consistency. Photogrammetry delivered information which hadn’t been collected with

other devices but was constrained by environmental conditions. Only through a

structured integration of these techniques - supported by robust planning phase and

workflows and clear scope of works - the full digital surveying can provide the most

suitable information.

2.6 Challenges and Limitations in data processing: A Critical
Overview

The objective of the data processing was to generate highly accurate, reliable, and up-

to-date information regarding the selected case studies from the raw data acquired on

site. To achieve this, hybrid and comprehensive point clouds were produced, with the

specific intent of serving as a foundation for subsequent parametric modelling

workflows within BIM environment.

To obtain consistent and coherent point clouds, raw data collected during the survey

campaigns had to be systematically processed and structured. This involved two

principal operations: registration and optimization.

2.6.1 Registration of the acquired data

Registration can be conducted either manually or through automated algorithms; in the

present work, the latter approach was adopted to enhance efficiency and ensure

consistent results. The registration process typically relies on aligning the relative

positions and orientations of the various scans, beginning with an approximate

alignment and subsequently refining it within a common coordinate system (Cheng et

al., 2018).

The specific registration workflow may vary depending on the acquisition technology

and devices used. In this research, consistent software tools were utilized across all

surveyed datasets, according to whether the data were acquired via TLS (either static

and mobile) or photogrammetric methods.

Static TLS data workflow - Datasets from static TLS were processed using Autodesk

ReCap. To reduce computational effort, scans from each case study were divided into

two distinct groups: one for external areas and one for internal spaces. These scans

were imported into the software in the chronological order in which they were acquired

during the fieldwork.

The registration process consisted of two main phases. The first phase involved coarse

registration, which estimated correspondences between adjacent scans based on

identifiable features. This phase enabled the preliminary alignment of the datasets. The

second phase involved fine registration, aimed at improving geometric accuracy. This

was achieved through the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm, the most widely

adopted method for aligning 3D point clouds. The ICP algorithm iteratively minimized

the distance between corresponding points in overlapping areas of different scans, and

in this context, it enabled an alignment accuracy in the range of a few millimetres, with

an average point spacing of about 3.5 mm (having the same point been surveyed in

many scans, with overabundant measurements).

Despite its effectiveness, the ICP algorithm has shown limitations, particularly in

transitional areas between interior and exterior environments (such as passageways

in the churches of San Giovanni dei Lebbrosi and Santissima Trinità di Delia) and in

the tallest parts of the façades (i.e. the bell tower in San Giovanni dei Lebbrosi,

probably due to the inclination of the laser beam), where the alignment proved unstable

and unreliable leading to eventual misalignments.

To address these criticalities, two specific strategies were implemented. To sort the first

inconvenient, redundant scans were deliberately included in both internal and external

datasets. These overlapping scans increased the number of shared features across

different zones, improving the registration process in problematic areas and ensuring

better integration of the complete models. At the end of the process, once ensured the

correct alignment of the scans with no discrepancies or misalignments through a

2.6.2 Optimization of the registered data

These consistent clusters thus obtained had to be further optimised prior to being

correctly imported into the BIM environment: essential operations such as the

segmentation, subsampling, alignment of different datasets, retention of the consistent

translation were performed at the end of the registration to refine the final point clouds,

in order for them to match the desired precision and quality standards, and enhance

the interpretability of the generated models.

\3. The church of San Giovanni dei
Lebbrosi in Palermo

This church is the first estate built in an Arab-Norman style in Sicily, even before the

establishment of the French conquerors in the island. It introduced brand new

architectural features merging the late European Romanesque and the Islamic

constructive traditions; considering these novelties, it became a stylistic fore type not

only for the following religious buildings in northern-western Sicily, but for other coeval

assets.

Since this building had never been surveyed before through modern methodologies,

its digitisation was aimed above all at providing the most complete information and at

delivering a high-detailed digital replica with an up-to-date database. This latter tool

could be used to share and foster the awareness on this asset after having been filed

for entering the UNESCO World Heritage list.

3.1 - Historical background

The church of San Giovanni dei Lebbrosi likely dates to 1071 when the Normans ranks,

led by Robert and Roger I of Altavilla, besieged Palermo to end Arab rule. It was first

built as a temporary structure near the ruins of a Saracen castle named “Yahya” (John

in Arabic) (Bellafiore, 1990). After the Normans conquered the island in 1072, king

Roger I turned it into a permanent church as a vow for his victory. By 1085, it was fully

built and is considered the first Arab-Norman structure in Sicily, showing early

architectural elements but lacking the refinements of later buildings (Valenti, 1921).

In 1155, king Roger II ordered a hospital next to the church to house leprosy patients

from St. Leonard’s church, keeping them away from the old town to prevent disease

spread. In 1219, emperor Frederick II handed control of the church and hospital to the

Teutonic Knights, who managed them until 1495, when the hospital was moved back

to the city due to a plague outbreak.

During the 17th and 18th centuries, the church underwent Baroque modifications. The

bell tower was altered, and a sacristy was added, partially blocking the porch. The

façade was stuccoed, and decorative elements were introduced for uniformity. Inside,

3.2 - Architectural description

San Giovanni dei Lebbrosi stands in the southern suburbs of Palermo, surrounded by

a green garden with remnants of the Saracen castle of Yahya (Bellafiore, 1990). The

main façade (west side) features a large round arch at the southern corner, leading to

a small porch with a pointed cross vault and the main entrance. Above it, Valenti’s bell

tower rises with elegant arched windows and a dome inspired by the original one above

the presbytery.

The side walls (north and south) are plain, with five pointed arched windows on each

side, decorated with recessed mouldings and stuccoed grills (musharabyas) based on

Islamic designs. The northern side has a stepped outline due to the higher presbytery

and stairwell sections, whilst the southern side ends with the presbytery. Additional

arched doors lead into the lateral naves. Stairwell is characterised by the presence of

slits cut in the walls.

At the rear, the three apses appear as simple curved walls with windows and

masharabyas. The presbytery, apses, and stairwell have flat, pinkish mortar-covered

roofs, whilst the naves have traditional pitched tile roofs. The domes, though now red,

were originally whitish, following North African Fatimid influences (Figure 12).

4.1 - Historical background

The Benedictine monastic complex of San Giovanni degli Eremiti was built by King

Roger II between 1132 and 1148 near the Royal Palace, on the banks of the Kemonia

stream, in an area with pre-existing structures.

On the same site, a previous monastery founded in 581 by Pope Gregory the Great

was destroyed by the Saracens in 842. Nearby stood one of Palermo’s nine city gates,

the bàb-al-abnà (“gate of the buildings” in Arabic), and a section of the city walls, still

partially visible today. Roger II designated the site as a burial place for his court

dignitaries in 1148, enriching the church with a dormitory, refectory, chapter house, and

abbot’s residence. Following Benedictine self-sufficiency rules, a mill and shops were

added in 1166. The monastery was fortified in 1325 during the Angevin siege. By 1464,

it was in decline and repopulated with monks from San Martino delle Scale.

Over the centuries, various modifications altered the original Arab-Norman structure.

In 1593, King Charles V transformed the Islamic Hall into a church dedicated to the

Virgin Mary. Further expansions, including a chapel dedicated to St. John the

Evangelist in 1606, modified the church layout (Torregrossa, 2013).

By 1739, Antonino Mongitore described its enlarged structure, additional naves, and

vaulted ceilings. In that description, the monastery and the refectory are also

mentioned for the first time, built immediately behind the N-W corner of the church

(completely covering the apses) along today's Via dei Benedettini and in the area

corresponding to the current hanging garden, connecting the religious building to the

Abbot's house, enlarged in 1834 (Mongitore, 1739).

The monastery fell into disrepair and was taken over by the state in 1865. Restoration

efforts began in 1876 under Giuseppe Patricolo, who reconstructed the central apse,

strengthened the bell tower, and restored architectural elements. A significant

discovery during this process was a well-preserved plaster masharabya, serving as a

model for similar reconstructions (Bellafiore, 1990).

During the demolition of the chapels of San Giovanni and the Virgin, Giuseppe

Patricolo discovered structural anomalies in the Norman church, including misaligned

walls and detached sections. Scraping off plaster revealed remnants of five cross

vaults and column stubs, leading Patricolo to hypothesize that the space had once

The third case study, the Church of Santissima Trinità di Delia or Cuba di Delia in

Castelvetrano (Trapani), was selected for its combination of Arab-Norman typological

features with unique innovations which make it different from the other previous two

coeval buildings. Its most distinctive aspect is its stereometry, as it follows a central

scheme based on a simple square for both the plan and elevations, resulting in an

almost perfect cube. This design reflects the influence of Greek Orthodox churches in

Sicily since the 7th century (the so-called “Cube”), later embraced by Arabic culture for

their geometric abstraction. Additionally, this church stands out as one of the few Arab-

Norman structures built outside Palermo, providing a valuable comparison with more

renowned examples.

5.1 - Historical background

The church of Santissima Trinità was built in the ancient fief of Delia, now part of

Castelvetrano's countryside in northwestern Sicily. While its exact construction date is

unknown, stylistic similarities to 12th-century Sicilian churches (San Niccolò la Regale

in Mazara del Vallo, 1124; Santa Maria dell’Ammiraglio in Palermo, 1143; San Giovanni

degli Eremiti in Palermo, 1142-1148) suggest it was built around 1140 (Di Stefano,

1979).

The first recorded mention of the church dates to 1392, when its administration was

entrusted to the prior of Delia, including responsibility for maintenance. Major and

arbitrary renovations occurred in 1527 and 1742: the former reinforced the vaults and

added battlements on top of the roof, giving the church a fortress-like appearance,

whilst the latter assessed earthquake damage, leading to structural reinforcements and

modifications, such as the main access, which was moved to the central apse, contrary

to its current appearance (Volpe, 2007).

Following the abolition of religious orders in Sicily in 1862, the church became private

property and was acquired by the Saporito Ricca family in 1866 (Calabrese Liotta and

5.2 - Architectural description

The term "Cuba," of uncertain etymology (the most accredited hypotheses trace the

word back to the Arabic equivalents qubbah = dome, kabah = cube or to the Latin cupa

\= barrel), refers to a type of religious building in Sicily dating back to the 7th century.

These structures typically have a square central layout, thick walls, small openings, at

least one eastern-facing apse, and a dome. The Santissima Trinità di Delia follows this

model with Arab-Norman influences. It has a nearly square plan (about 10 m per side)

with three pronounced eastern apses and a total height of nearly 13 m at the dome’s

extrados.

Externally, the façades reflect the interior structure, divided into two height sections.

The lower part is flat, while the upper features mouldings framing three pointed arches

windows per façade. The roof is structured into different-levelled terraces, highlighting

Patricolo added a plaster layer for waterproofing, composed by lime mortar, quarry

recessed mouldings, whilst the masharabyas were modern additions by Patricolo,

covered by barrel vaults, whilst the corner bays have cross vaults (Figure 53). The

6.1 - Historical background

The Church of Santa Maria della Grotta is part of a wider thousand-year-old 4,400 m²

archaeological complex in Marsala, Italy, containing remains from the Punic, Roman,

and Medieval periods. Located within the Phoenician necropolis of Capo Boeo (4th

century B.C.), the church is partly carved into rock. Fully uncovered in 1888, the site

revealed vertical-shaft burial pits, some later adapted as skylights for the underlying

hypogea. In the 3rd century A.D., during Roman rule, the settlement of Lilybaeum (the

ancient name of Marsala) became a thriving colony, and the area was converted into

a sandstone quarry (latomie), later serving as catacombs for Paleo-Christian and

Jewish communities by the 5th century. In 1097, under the orders of King Roger I, a

Greek Orthodox monastic community established a coenobitic abbey within the former

catacombs, dedicating it to the Virgin Mary. A fortified tower, later transformed into a

bell tower, marked the entrance (Caruso, 1995). By the 15th century, the church

changed hands multiple times, first managed by the Diocese of Palermo and later by

the Jesuits, who commissioned Giovan Biagio Amico (1712–1715) to redesign it in a

late Baroque style (Figure 67).

6.2 - Architectural description

The current church, approximately 31 meters long and 10 meters wide, is built over a

\7. The HBIM modelling: from a parametric
approach to the creation of a typological
library

Once obtained the datasets from the digital surveys and subsequent processing to be

used as the most reliable basis for the parametric modelling phase, the HBIM

digitisation of the case studies in this research was aimed at turning the geometric

information contained in the point clouds into recognizable structural items, capable of

describing the whole architectural systems and the features of single elements in their as-

built conditions, dimensions and functions.

An effective digital replica from a point cloud information should provide an adequate

representation of the real historical assets including their peculiarities, and a

reproduction of their logical structures as consistent as possible (Adami et al., 2019).

The digital reconstruction of the case studies followed this principle. Each building was

analysed and decomposed through a top-down investigation, from an overall to an

elementary level, in order to understand the role of each item in its broader context,

how they have been built and connected to other parts in terms of hierarchies and

spatial relationships. The parametric modelling was performed in a BIM environment

using Autodesk Revit. This software was chosen because it efficiently handles point

clouds and has an intuitive interface. It is also a powerful database which can catalogue

all 2D and 3D elements along with necessary semantic information for modelling.

Although the process took more time, the modelling followed a “direct method”,

segmenting the point cloud into horizontal and vertical 2D planes to clarify the

buildings' structure and spatial organization. Automated or semi-automated methods

7.1 The creation of a typological library: a methodological
approach

The research methodology presented in this chapter is structured into four stages

(Figure 91), beginning with the identification of a key issue: a gap in the knowledge

and in the application of the potentialities offered by BIM tools to AH assets.

As previously discussed on page 15, BIM’s parametric libraries were primarily

developed for standardised, contemporary construction and tailored to prefabricated

elements. Maurice Murphy’s 2012 proposal to adapt digital libraries specifically for AH

\-using point clouds and stylistic rules architectural essays- marked a significant step

forward (Murphy, 2012). Nevertheless, existing HBIM libraries still remain narrow in

scope, often focusing mainly on classical or Renaissance European architecture
(Murphy, 2012; Apollonio et al., 2012; Kontoudaki and Georgopoulos, 2022; Sakellaris
et al., 2022; Daniels and Georgopoulos, 2023) and struggling to represent irregular

geometries.

Relatively to the models shown in this chapter, the research tried to partially address

this gap through an extensive literature review and selected case studies on Islamic

and Middle Eastern architecture, offering valuable insights into Arab structural systems
and decorative elements (Khalid et al., 2024; Benli et al., 2024; Zeitouni et Asslan,

2025).

The second stage defined the project’s main objective: to produce digital models which

support knowledge dissemination and raise awareness of Arab-Norman heritage,

particularly relevant after the inclusion of one of these case studies in the UNESCO

World Heritage List in 2015 and another’s nomination.

The models were developed with a purely geometric focus, aiming to replicate the as-

built architectural, structural, and decorative features of the real buildings as faithfully

as possible for visualization, 3D documentation, and graphic analysis purposes. Whilst

the models incorporate basic semantic data (e.g., materials, construction phases from

standard software databases), further semantic enrichment was intentionally omitted

due to the complexity of standardising heterogeneous historical elements and this

The modelled elements were developed with a Level of Geometry (LOG) appropriate

to the corresponding LOD whenever a semantic content had been attributed, with the

aim of balancing representational fidelity and modelling efficiency.

In particular, three different LOGs have been chosen, according to AIA standards:

✓ LOG 300: Generic elements from standard libraries (e.g., walls, floors, ceilings)

✓ LOG 350: Custom loadable families for typical Arab-Norman features (e.g., domes,

windows, vaults)

✓ LOG 400: Detailed elements modelled in place or as families (e.g., trumpet or

spherical pendentives, musharabyas)

The proposed solution was developed according to a modelling strategy based on

Shape Grammar, which relies on creating libraries of parametric objects based on

architectural principles, through computer algorithms (which are not included in this

dissertation) and encoded rule sets derived from historical manuscripts.

The case studies have been investigated through quantitative and qualitative analyses

aimed at defying the spatial and organizational relationships occurring between the

different objects, according to three different approaches:

\- global: this was applied to the buildings on their whole, to establish geometrical and

mathematical rules (such as reference plans, symmetries, max/min. values, mutual

proportions) involving the placement and the composition of the architectural items into

a measurable space;

\- macro-areal: for understanding the hierarchical structure, the arrangement and the

behaviour of spatial classes (naves, spans, supports, etc.) connected by topological

relationships;

\- elementary: regarding single items in a unit (columns, walls, arches, etc.), affecting

the morphology of their primary and secondary components;

Shape Grammar has then been subsequently tailored to the tools made available by

BIM software, where the modelling withstood the formal scheme of the families defined

Parameters have been used to enable rapid, flexible, and efficient adjustments to

7.2 Preliminary operations and best practises

For each case study, a new architectural project was filed in Revit, and the

corresponding point cloud from the data processing was imported through the "Insert"

panel. In this step, it was important to choose the “import from origin to internal origin”

option to ensure proper alignment and consistency between the point clouds and the

project’s reference system. After importing, the point cloud was locked to avoid

accidental movement during modelling and used as a "scaffold" for building the model

upon (Martinelli et al., 2022).

The first task was to identify the construction planes of the actual buildings, such as

the levels of floors, roofs, arch springers, and main axes, based on the point cloud

(Figure 92). After setting the main sections through the dedicated command in the

“View” panel, reference grids and levels were created from the “Architecture” ribbon.

These helped with the spatial placement of construction elements and provided

geometric constraints for structural modelling.

7.3 The modelling of parametric objects: system families

After these initial settings, the architectural elements were modelled following a top-

down approach, starting with the simpler and progressing to the more complex ones.

The modelling began with the parametric objects, which were already provided by

default in the software. Their families were edited using a family > family type >

instance structure to create customized objects which correspond to the real

structures, and these were integrated into the Revit relational database. Examples of

these parametric objects include floors, walls, ceilings, and roofs.

Floors – The modelling began with the internal floors of the buildings. Vertical sections

through the point clouds revealed that no floor had continuous structural alignment

(e.g., the presbytery areas were raised, and at San Giovanni degli Eremiti, several

levels were present throughout the building). To accurately represent their slopes, an

appropriate strategy was devised. Initially, floor portions were modelled as horizontal

using the “floor” system family.

Their outlines were traced based on the perimeter from the point cloud at the levels

corresponding to their finished surface. Since only the upper decking was known, and

there was no information on the thickness or layers of the floor, a conventional variable

thickness was applied to match the external ground planes. A top layer of 3 cm,

representing terracotta tiles on a 2 cm bed, was assumed.

identified. Their elevation was measured by calculating the height difference between

these points and the horizontal reference plane of the floor. These points were then

placed on the corresponding elements in the model using the “add point” command,

with their elevation adjusted using the “edit sub-element” command. This allowed the

floors to be divided into smaller triangular elements which reflected the profiles defined

by the slopes (Figure 93).

Walls – The walls in the digital models were classified into two categories: straight and

curved. Straight walls were the majority, whilst curved walls were used for the apses.

plans and sections, respecting their base and top levels as constraints, along with their

thickness.

A singularity was found in Santissima Trinità di Delia, where the perimetral walls had

an overhang profile outward. In this case, an intermediate level was traced in

correspondence of the changing dimensions, then the lower and the upper walls have

been treated as separate units.

Throughout the modelling process, the “Connect” option was kept active to join walls

together, ensuring a consistent perimeter. Due to historical construction techniques,

some walls were not perfectly orthogonal, but this was addressed by turning off the

snap option for more precise tracing. Fortunately, the point clouds did not reveal

significant tapering, and the walls displayed consistent thicknesses.

7.5 Other examples of in-place objects

Although system families and ad hoc parameterization effectively replicated most Arab-

Norman architectural elements, some unique shapes remain which cannot be

modelled using the parametric tools and techniques described so far.

These include spherical pendentives, apse basins, curved apse windows and

mashrabiyas. Modelled as in-place elements, they have two drawbacks: they are

exclusive to a specific project, which prevents any possibility of creating adaptable

families and being reused; furthermore, while being geometrically accurate, they do

not contain any additional semantic data. It is possible to assign them only one

material, but not the potential stratigraphy which that element is composed of.

Spherical Pendentives - Unlike trumpet-shaped pendentives, spherical ones cannot

be parameterized in Revit due to their double-curved, triangular shape. To model them,

the relevant portions of the point cloud were manually segmented in Cloud Compare

and exported as .ply files into Rhinoceros, being careful to set meters as the unit of

measurement.

After identifying the three edge lines and vertices of a pendentive, the external

perimeter was drawn with the "curve" command. Edge curves were then created to

generate the double-curved surface using "create surface from edge curves." This

element was replicated seven times to form the connection with the circular-plan

domes above. The pendentives were exported as .dwg files, imported as masses into

Revit, and placed correctly. Using the "Roof by surface" command from the "Massing

& Site" panel, the digital model was finalized, assigning a 1 cm thickness with exposed

sandstone (Figure 113).

Apse basins - Like spherical pendentives, apse basins are double-curved surfaces

and lateral apses and are characterized by a semicircular plan with a pointed-arch

vertical section.

Whilst the apses were modelled as curved walls (or partially curved, as in San Giovanni

the springers of the basins, the wall family could not be used for the basins themselves.

Instead, an unconventional but effective strategy was adopted: representing them as

solid, single-layer ceilings in sandstone, from which the hollow basin was then

subtracted.

the overhanging flat roof level and the springer level. Their perimeter was derived from

a horizontal view of the point cloud at the keystone level of each basin. The hollow

portion was modelled as a local component, obtained through a void sweep. The main

profile followed the generator curve of the pointed arch from a cross-section, whilst the

path corresponded to the wall’s curvature.

8.5 The current state of the art of decay mapping in HBIM

A significant challenge in preserving AH lies in the lack or loss of detailed information

about the asset in question - how it was originally built and its restoration history. This

gap becomes particularly problematic when planning future maintenance or

intervention strategies.

Traditional archival research and 2D documentation methods for existing structures

have proven both labor-intensive and incomplete, because of their inconsistency in

framing discontinuous and insufficiently documented previous maintenance

campaigns; whilst effective management of BH requires updated and structured tools

to thoroughly understand historical assets and ensure their sustainability over time

(Santagati et al., 2021).

Beyond standard 2D graphical representations, thematic mappings can also be

integrated into HBIM systems as proper 3D entities (He et al., 2017). Indeed, numerous

researchers have explored ways to embed this type of semantic data into the database

169

<!-- p. 186 -->

The HBIM modelling for a thematic mapping

of digital models resulting from Scan-to-BIM processes, with varying methods and

outcomes, including VPL language (Guida et al., 2021).

One commonly used technique involves tracing decay contours using filled patterns

(Chiabrando et al., 2017), though this method lacks data on intervention quantities and

costs. A similar approach utilizes thin-layered filled regions on affected surfaces,

enabling the addition of semantic metadata (Croce et al., 2019). This information can

even further detail the material's conservation state, describe specific interventions

(Ferro et al., 2023), or highlight structural issues and prioritize areas requiring attention

(Saricaoglu and Saygi, 2022).

More advanced techniques involve adaptive, contoured patterns linked to different

types of decay. For instance, one approach utilized identification codes, although it did

not facilitate data extraction (Malinverni et al., 2019). Alternatively, this methodology

has been applied to individual masonry ashlars for greater precision (Del Pozzo et al.,

2022), or a single architectural element (Nieto-Julián et al., 2024).

Some researchers have also integrated orthophotos into models, allowing for direct

tracing of decay areas (Lanzara et al., 2021). These areas are subsequently analysed

in a separate database outside the BIM environment used for modelling (Banfi et al.,

2022).

its condition, often derived from visual inspections, non-destructive diagnostic tests

conducted on-site, or laboratory analyses of sampled materials.

Discussions and Conclusions

HBIM processes are established as a powerful tool for the preservation, restoration,

and enhancement of Architectural Heritage. The HBIM models act as integrated digital

archives, storing semantic and geometric data; they can be explored, exploited,

updated and revised at all times, and enable further queries, analysis, and simulations,

supporting informed decision-making processes.

Interoperable and open-source platforms allow data from diverse software to be stored

within one environment and shared among professionals and stakeholders, fostering

awareness and promoting collaborative and team-oriented methods of work.

Given the notorious lack of information affecting the historical existing buildings,

nowadays HBIM relies on a Scan-to-BIM approach to address this issue through the

methodical use of modern Geomatics techniques.

The main objective of this research was to validate the Scan-to-BIM approach as an

effective method for the documentation, representation and communication of AH, to

support and to be integrated into HBIM methodology.

For this purpose, the Scan-to-BIM approach was tested on three magnificent built

examples of the Arab-Norman Heritage and on a Baroque church in western Sicily,

trying to establish and implement an efficient and replicable workflow for the geometric

and semantic reconstruction of historical assets, and assessing the advantages,

limitations, and criticalities of the application of this approach for modelling existing

buildings.

The work has been structured to endorse the assumption that in-depth knowledge

about the buildings should have been compulsory to develop more effective HBIM

models aimed at sharing improved awareness on those assets.

Prior to starting any operational phase of the workflow, it was essential to deepen the

understanding of the historical and architectural context of the case studies through

archival and bibliographic research. These investigations initially allowed for an

analysis on their evolution over the centuries, as well as the identification of any

interventions aimed at restoring original conditions. Subsequently, they contributed to

understanding the specific characteristics of each architectural element -especially

from a technological-structural, constructional, and material perspective- with the goal

of reflecting and conveying these aspects throughout the modelling process; in

particular, they aided the aggregation and hierarchy of the various parts within the

models establishing their mutual relationships.

From a purely geometric standpoint, the accurate and detailed documentation of the

sites in their actual condition was achieved through the integration of diverse survey

techniques, in an effort to overcome the limitations of each method and to produce

datasets which are as complete, homogeneous, and consistent with the aims of the

Scan-to-BIM process as possible, not only for conservation purposes.

From a methodological perspective, all surveys in this research were conducted using

two main techniques: terrestrial laser scanning (both static and mobile) and

photogrammetry (both terrestrial and aerial, when possible). Additionally, topographic

surveying was marginally employed to acquire specific control points, which supported

data processing and improved overall accuracy.

All the collected data, coming from the investigation process and the surveys, served

as a reliable basis for replicating geometrical information during the modelling phase,

albeit they needed to be optimized and interpreted prior to being uploaded into

parametric models.

During the subsequential development of the HBIM digitisations for the case studies,

a crucial phase was the decomposition of the architecture into its structural and formal

components, followed by the reconstruction carried out exclusively through manual

processes.

The parametric reconstruction process highlighted the effectiveness of the survey

techniques, but also the challenges of modelling elements which inevitably had to take

into account the rigidity of the tools offered by the BIM platform. In fact, the in-built

system families and predefined libraries within BIM software typically include only

parametric objects suitable for newly constructed buildings and -except in rare cases-

do not account for elements characteristic of historical architecture. Whilst these

system families were essential in the early stages of the process and in the modelling

of primary elements such as floors, walls, and ceilings, the main challenge was to

define modelling strategies capable of representing the unique features of Arab-

Norman and Baroque architecture.

Thanks to the editor function and the available templates, through manual drafting and

Boolean operations, it was possible to parameterize most of the typical elements of

those architectural styles: the main dimensions of the most recurring profiles were

made customizable, and the resulting model was saved as a downloadable family, to

be imported into future projects and altered as needed. This approach enabled the

creation of reusable families, such as windows, vaults, domes, etc.

However, a residual portion of architectural elements was created as local families,

and thus usable only within a specific project, prioritizing model accuracy at the

expense of parametrization.

Although based on shared modelling processes, the HBIM digitisations in this research

pursued different objectives, which justify their varying degrees of geometric and

semantic contents, as well as their levels of development.

In particular, the models related to the first three case studies -given their stylistic and

chronological homogeneity- were suitable for creating and implementing a brand new

library of Arab-Norman architectural types (arches, windows with molded frames,

trumpet squinches, domes), contributing to greater awareness of the structural aspects

and specific features of the three churches, in line with the principles of AH

documentation. The rigor of archival research ensured critical elaboration processes

throughout their modelling.

Semantic contents in the form of a thematic mapping for degradation have been added

to the fourth case study only. The digital replica of Santa Maria della Grotta

demonstrated how the Scan-to-BIM process can be a valuable tool for analysing the

building’s current state of preservation and for conducting investigations as an

essential foundation for conservation efforts and scheduled maintenance. Within that

model, detailed analysis was carried out on the various forms of decay affecting the

church, through mapping conducted on both faces of the main walls. This first

experiment overcame the usual bidimensional representation of one side of the wall at

a time, to be redone from scratch whenever updated: in this case, the extension of

affected surfaces can be altered on demand and stored and enables both a

multitemporal visualisation through a frame of time, and the contemporary inspection

of different faces of the same element.

The default database provided by the BIM environment has been expanded and

enriched with abacuses reporting extensions, causes, descriptions and locations on

ad-hoc sheets, and can be further expanded in the future, for example by integrating

restoration techniques, costs or other specificities related to AH management.

After these considerations, the Scan-to-BIM approach applied to the case studies in

this research demonstrated significant advantages in documenting the actual

conditions of the architectural artifacts and in creating parametric models which serve

as digital replicas.

The digital surveys, conducted for the first time ever on the artifacts studied,

contributed significantly to their comprehensive and thorough documentation. The

resulting datasets, beyond their potential use as a base for HBIM modelling, represent

a valuable outcome on their own.

Furthermore, the research has proven how integrated, multi-source surveys combining

mobile and aerial and terrestrial photogrammetry, can effectively support the

development of accurate 3D digital reconstruction of monumental architectures

especially whenever made of organic parts as in the fourth case study.

The parameterization of irregular geometries through manual methods, although

directly related to the purposes for which the models were created, has been overcome

thanks to the analysis of the most recurring shapes and their redesign and led to two

libraries of objects related to the Arab-Norman and Baroque styles, completely new in

their kind. In fact, available libraries of historical objects were usually limited to those

associated with the classical and neoclassical orders of architecture. This represents

another result achieved through this research.

The latter HBIM model, instead, enabled further consideration on the retrieval of

information from professionals with different backgrounds, and how this information

could have improved collaborative work and multidisciplinary.

Despite increasingly efficient management, some issues remain unresolved: whilst this

research has proposed several strategies to address the lack of complete geometric

and architectural documentation and the challenges of modelling historic buildings,

often characterized by complex forms, there are still critical concerns. These include

the limited interoperability between data shared on the hosting platform when

originating from different software, and the absence of a common vocabulary and clear

guidelines, which continue to hinder the broader adoption of the Scan-to-BIM

approach.

The digital models produced as part of this doctoral research do not resolve all the

challenges, but they have proven to be valuable tools for the meaningful enhancement

and management of real-world artifacts, in an effort to achieve the most comprehensive

understanding possible.
