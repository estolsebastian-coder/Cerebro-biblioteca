INTRODUCTION
The Watu Baula statues at the Tantaduo site in the Bada Valley constitute a megalithic
heritage complex within Lore Lindu National Park, a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve in
Central Sulawesi (Tempo, 2020). Located about 60 km south of Palu, the park spans roughly
217,000 ha at 1,700–2,000 m asl and contains hundreds of megalithic objects. The Watu
Baula statues (Figure 1), tentatively dated to around 2000 BC, are regarded among the
earliest megalithic remains in Indonesia (Kusumo, 2021, 2023; Nuraedah et al., 2023;
Tambalo, 2018). Beyond their archaeological value, they function as sign systems that
mediate social meaning, identity, cosmology, and spatial practice across generations.
Semiotic approaches, including Peircean analysis, treat artifacts and spatial arrangements as
icons, indices, and symbols within community meaning-making. In the digital context,
“digital semiotics” (O’Halloran, 2023a) extends this reading through 3D representations,

Digital semiotic approaches open new possibilities for linking form, function, and meaning
through replicable digital data for example, detecting semiotic structures in prehistoric art with
computational linguistics and aligning digital and physical representations that “re-present”
kinship relationships between artifacts in exhibition spaces (Swenson & Cipolla, 2020).
Advances in photogrammetry, particularly Structure-from-Motion (SfM), have made 3D
documentation faster, more cost-effective, and more accurate, and can be integrated with
terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) to produce high-fidelity models (Barszcz et al., 2021). Recent
work also positions photogrammetry as standard practice in conserving buildings and cultural
artifacts: TLS photogrammetry workflows support historical analysis and deformation
monitoring while expanding the evidentiary base from surface textures that preserve traces of
craftsmanship and iconography to volumetric geometry for assessing layout and spatial
relationships (Xing et al., 2025).

LITERATURE REVIEW AND PREVIOUS RESEARCH
In the context of megalithic objects, photogrammetry has been applied to inscribed and carved
monuments to document rock art, map damage, and support iconographic interpretation, as
shown in spatial–geographic heritage studies by Blettery et al. (2021) and De Luca (2019;
2010). Recent work on dolmens, menhirs, and megalithic complexes demonstrates that
combining SfM, TLS, and micro-precision imaging (photometric stereo) yields detailed 3D
models for reading motifs, carving techniques, and damage patterns, opening the way for more
precise semiotic analyses of megalithic “form” as a digital visual language (Peña-Villasenín
et al., 2024). In Indonesia, the megalithic landscape is rich yet still under-documented in terms
of high-fidelity 3D records integrated with interpretation. Ansori et al. (2023), for example,
highlight in the Karangsambung–Karangbolong Geopark the need for mapping methods
sensitive to diverse cultural remains from megalithic to colonial periods, aligning with
agendas of 3D documentation and cross-scale semiotic reading. Within this context, the Watu

and heritage: Skrede (2023) offers a socio-semiotic reading of monument-like artifacts
(Stolpersteine), showing how location, material, and public interaction shape meaning; Smith
(2016) examines how visuals of cultural heritage for tourists are produced and perceived,
emphasizing digital semiotics in visitor experience; and Tan et al. (2018) employ a
multimodal, computation-based approach to online image collections, demonstrating how
digital analysis can strengthen the validity of semiotic interpretation.
In the 3D domain, literature confirms that 2D/3D modeling has become an analytical tool that
generates new questions about patterns, spatial relations, and affordances precisely what is
needed to bridge photogrammetric data and semiotic argumentation. On this basis, a digital
semiotic study is proposed that contributes at three stages: (1) methodologically, integrating
the photogrammetric pipeline (acquisition–reconstruction–validation) with a digital semiotic
framework; (2) empirically, enriching local megalith documentation with high-quality,
reusable 3D models for research and teaching; and (3) practically, supporting more
participatory management and dissemination of megalithic heritage (e.g., 3D/VR replicas for
public education and conservation planning). Recent literature indicates that such an
ecosystem is technically feasible and impactful for conservation, curatorial work, and crosscommunity learning (Stylianidis et al., 2022). The Tantaduo site, part of the broader Central
Sulawesi megalithic landscape, is chosen as a test bed to connect photogrammetric outputs
with semiotic interpretations (form–meaning–practice). To link 3D form data with cultural
meaning, this study develops a workflow beginning with photogrammetric measurement
(acquisition, modeling, validation), then mapping findings via the SF-MDA framework, and
finally testing spatial patterns through Spatial Grammar and viewpoint simulations. Three
research questions guide the analysis: RQ1 examines recurring size orientation distance
patterns and their geometric reliability; RQ2 translates 3D metrics into technical ritual
meanings, social relations, and experiential flows; and RQ3 verifies center satellite patterns,
processional chains, and performative fields, and their implications for curation and
preservation. Each stage is thus mutually reinforcing: RQ1 secures robust measurements, RQ2
links metrics to meaning, and RQ3 tests their applicability for management decisions at the
Tantaduo site.

RESEARCH METHODS
This study integrates photogrammetric metrics (length, width, height, orientation, distance,
and relief) with digital semiotic analysis SF MDA (ideational, interpersonal, and textual
metafunctions) and Spatial Grammar to translate 3D forms into auditable spatial relations and
cultural meanings. Its novelty lies in an integrated metric–semiotic pipeline, following
Halliday and O’Halloran (2021; 2023b), for coding and interpreting semiotic meaning in 3D
form, spatial composition, and materiality. The analysis is supported by computational
methods including visual analytics, geometric measurement, and multimodal annotation,
enabling systematic qualitative–quantitative integration (Froideval et al., 2019; PeñaVillasenín et al., 2024). The research is conducted in the megalithic landscape of Lore Lindu
National Park, Central Sulawesi, specifically at the Tantaduo site in the Bada Valley (Bada–
Besoa–Napu cluster). The Watu Balua anthropomorphic statue is selected as the case study
due to its accessibility, preservation, and morphological representativeness (Table 1).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Field measurements of the Tatanduo Statue (Watu Baula) indicate dimensions of 330 cm
(length), 180 cm (width), and 90 cm (height). The photogrammetric reconstruction based on
the Sketchfab model yields corresponding dimensions of 335 cm, 196 cm, and 89 cm. Relative
to the reference measurements (height assumed at 85 cm), the model shows deviations of +5
cm in length (1.5%), +16 cm in width (8.9%), and +4 cm in height (4.7%), resulting in an
overall RMSE of 9.95 cm. A sensitivity test using alternative reference heights yields RMSE
values of 10.98 cm (80 cm) and 9.70 cm (90 cm), with the latter reducing the height error to
−1 cm. Error decomposition indicates that width accounts for the dominant contribution to
the total squared error (256 of 297; ~86%), making it the primary source of geometric
discrepancy. This distortion affects the shape ratio, with L/W shifting from 1.83 in the field
data to 1.71 in the model, corresponding to a relative change of approximately 6–7%
compared with the values reported in Tables 2 and 3.

for the observed variations in surface texture and preservation.

To avoid fragmented readings of form, layers of meaning are first structured through the
genre–register–discourse framework. This approach allows material features such as relief,
orientation, proportion, and periodic shadow—to be interpreted as purposeful social
practice involving actors, functions, and communicative media. Building on the SF-MDA
framework (Table 6), ideational, interpersonal, and textual indicators are mapped onto the
local cultural context, positioning the 3D model of the Watu Baula statue not merely as
geometric documentation but as a text that produces, negotiates, and stratifies meaning
(Biber & Conrad, 2019; Handford & Gee, 2023).

B. Stratification Of Meaning: Genre, Register, And Cultural Discourse
• Ritual–memorial genre. The orientation and centrality of the Watu Baula statue,
together with the defined entrance area, indicate its function as a locus for ancestral
commemoration. The spatial sequence follows a clear progression: entrance → transition
→ ceremonial center → closure.
• Visitor engagement in preservation. Preservation at the Tantaduo site integrates ritual
leaders, guardians, residents, and visitors under established etiquette. On-site
participation can be extended through complementary digital access—such as virtual
tours and annotated maps—designed to enhance, not substitute, embodied engagement.
• Conservation discourse. As a cultural heritage asset, the Watu Baula statue requires
contemporary conservation strategies grounded in its scientific, educational, and identity-
forming value. Photogrammetry not only documents form but also articulates meaning,
informing decisions on appropriate preservation approaches.

C. Spatial Grammar on the Tantaduo Site
The spatial grammar of the Tantaduo megalithic site indicates that the ritual space of the
Watu Baula monolith is structured by three elements. First, a central pattern positions Watu
Baula as a single monolith functioning as the axis of spatial orientation and the focal point
of ritual activity. Second, a processional chain is articulated through boundary markers that
organize sequential actions approach, pause, orientation, and return so that the open space
operates not merely as a container, but as a scripted sequence of movement. Third, a
performative field emerges from the flat–concave ground surface and the open forecourt,
providing interpersonal distance and acoustic conditions that support ritual speech.
Photogrammetry-based semiotic analysis contributes three foundations: navigable
accuracy, enabling geometric validation and visual access beyond in situ observation;
interoperability, allowing integration with 3D object data, archaeological records, and oral
archives to strengthen semiotic triangulation; and participation, through collaborative
annotation that enables local communities to inscribe names and narratives, transforming
the 3D model into a living, evolving text (Hanchard, 2024).

D. Synthesis: From Form to Cultural Meaning

The findings indicate that the east–west orientation and the statues’ proportional
module (4:1) function not merely as geometric features but as technical–ritual rules
regulating the timing and conduct of ritual practice. Interpersonally, scale and elevation
establish a prescribed viewing position that reinforces symbolic authority; textually, the
spatial sequence threshold → transition → center organizes embodied experience through
movement, micro-water flows, and cyclical shadow patterns. Within this framework,
digital annotation operates as a metadiscursive layer linking material evidence with
communal memory. The 3D reconstruction confirms that the Tantaduo megalithic form
follows recurring conventions of size, orientation, and distance rather than arbitrary
placement.Applying Halliday–O’Halloran’s digital semiotic framework, the
photogrammetric data articulate three metafunctions: ideational (incremental construction,
cosmic orientation, monolith erection), interpersonal (scale and height regulating viewing
distance and respectful comportment), and textual (spatial flow directing attention and
bodily movement). Digital annotation thus emerges as a new discourse capable of
integrating preservation, education, and identity without displacing locally embedded
meanings.

E. Regional global context: Watu Baula in The Broader Megalithic Tradition
The Watu Baula statue, located within the Lore Lindu megalithic complex (Bada–
Besoa–Napu), forms part of a dense ritual landscape comprising ancestral statues, kalamba
(stone burial jars), perforated flat stones, markers, and related features. This diversity
indicates that Watu Baula is not an isolated artefact but an integrated element of a broader
sacred spatial system. In comparative perspective, such configurations align with global
megalithic traditions in which stone monuments articulate socio-sacred boundaries and
structure ritual movement. The placement of Watu Baula may therefore be read as a
regional expression of a cross-cultural phenomenon: stone monuments functioning as
spatial texts that conjoin material form, orientation, and socio-ritual practice.

CONCLUSION
a. Empirical/Photogrammetric Conclusions
\- Photogrammetric reconstruction preserves the statue’s overall form and differentiates
tool marks from weathering, supporting conservation recommendations.
\- A +16 cm (8.9%) width deviation in the 3D model accounted for 86% of the MSE and
lowered the length:width ratio from 1.83 (original) to 1.71 (model), indicating the need for
tighter width control during scanning.
\- Surface relief (5–12 mm) is better preserved in the drier, sun-exposed upper area, while
the mud-submerged lower area shows stronger weathering; microenvironmental
differences shape texture, color, and preservation.
\- The 3D model confirms key megalithic features reclined monoliths, flat structures, and
boundary stones aligned predominantly east west, shaped by local contours and paddy-field
microflows.

b. Interpretive Conclusions (digital semiotic readings using SF-MDA)
\- Within the Halliday O’Halloran (SF-MDA) framework, the photogrammetric record
functions as a meaning-making system: ideational (staged construction, cosmic
orientation, monolith erection), interpersonal (scale and height structure viewing distance
and deference), and textual (spatial sequencing from boundary to transition to focal center).
\- The spatial grammar (center, processional chain, performative field) shows that statue
size, form, and orientation are not merely geometric; they follow technical-ritual
conventions that structure community practice and encode enduring cultural discourse

c. Practical implications & limitations.
The digital semiotics resulting from this research emphasizes the importance of arranging
viewing zones and tourist routes in accordance with the orientation and spatial proportions
of the Tantaduo site. The maintenance of the Watu Baula statue requires adherence to
protocols that preserve its original patina as a megalithic relic. In digital heritage, the
annotation, curation, and presentation of information about the statue must be managed
proportionally to prevent visitors from overinterpreting the site. The semiotic impact is the
creation of a digital experience that provides a context collectively understood by the
surrounding community, allowing visitors to independently understand the meaning by
reading the signs, space, and history of the megalithic objects at the Tantaduo site
